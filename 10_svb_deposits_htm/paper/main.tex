\begin{abstract}
\noindent When Silicon Valley Bank collapsed in March 2023, two narratives competed:
that banks failed because of \emph{unrealized losses} on long-duration securities, and
that they failed because \emph{uninsured depositors} ran. We separate the two in the
cross-section of bank equity returns. Using 165 publicly-traded U.S.\ banks, we compute
market-model cumulative abnormal returns (CARs) over the SVB window (March 8--13, 2023)
and relate them to two predetermined 2022Q4 balance-sheet exposures built from
bank-regulatory filings: the ratio of estimated uninsured deposits to assets, and unrealized
securities losses scaled by equity. The average bank lost 14.1\% over the window
($t=-13.7$). In the cross-section, a one-standard-deviation increase in the uninsured-deposit
ratio is associated with a 4.2-percentage-point larger equity loss ($t=-3.05$), robust to
controlling for size and to excluding the eight U.S.\ global systemically important banks.
The run channel survives every design we throw at it: a covariate-balanced matching estimator
($-5.4$pp, $t=-2.43$), randomization inference on 2{,}000 permutations of exposure
($p<0.001$), and a clean pre-event placebo window in which the uninsured gradient is flat
($t=-0.37$) even though banks were already falling together. The securities-loss (mark-to-market)
channel is, by contrast, fragile: it is insignificant under conventional robust inference in the
horse race ($t=-1.16$), only marginal under randomization inference ($p=0.04$), and about half
the magnitude of the run channel. A minimum-detectable-effect analysis shows the design is
well-powered for the run channel and can bound---though not sharply pin down---the smaller
mark-to-market channel. The market priced run risk far more than mark-to-market risk. Three
hand-collected refinements---correctly aggregated multi-bank uninsured deposits, a
bank-specific-news confound screen, and realized deposit outflows as a second outcome---are
reserved as the companion student contribution.\\[4pt]
\noindent\textbf{JEL:} G21, G14, G01, E58. \quad
\textbf{Keywords:} bank runs, uninsured deposits, unrealized losses, SVB, event study,
randomization inference, statistical power.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
In the second week of March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) went from solvent to
FDIC receivership in roughly 48 hours, and the equity of regional banks fell sharply.
Two explanations were offered almost immediately, and they carry different policy
implications. The first blames \emph{interest-rate risk}: after the fastest tightening
cycle in four decades, banks held large unrealized losses on held-to-maturity (HTM) and
available-for-sale (AFS) securities that, if marked to market, wiped out reported capital
\citep{jiang2024}. The second blames \emph{funding fragility}: banks financed by
\emph{uninsured} deposits---which are not protected by the FDIC and can flee at par---were
exposed to a classic run \citep{diamond1983,egan2017}. Because SVB was extreme on
\emph{both} dimensions, the raw failure does not tell us which exposure the market believed
was fatal.

This paper runs the horse race directly. We ask: across publicly-traded banks, did the
equity-price collapse in the SVB window load more on a bank's uninsured-deposit exposure
or on its hidden securities losses, holding size fixed? We answer with a standard
cross-sectional event study \citep{mackinlay1997}. For 165 banks we estimate market-model
CARs over March 8--13, 2023, and regress them on two exposures measured at 2022Q4---before
the run---so that the right-hand-side variables are predetermined and cannot be a mechanical
response to the crash itself.

Our results are clear and, we think, economically important. First, the shock was large
and broad: the average bank lost 14.1\% over the four-trading-day window
($t=-13.7$; Table~\ref{tab:cars}), an order of magnitude larger than the market-model
prediction. Second, and centrally, \emph{uninsured-deposit exposure wins the horse race}.
A one-standard-deviation increase in the uninsured-deposit ratio is associated with a
4.0--5.0-percentage-point larger CAR loss, with $t$-statistics between $-2.6$ and $-3.1$
across specifications (Table~\ref{tab:main}). The effect survives controlling for size and
is, if anything, slightly stronger when we drop the eight U.S.\ global systemically
important banks (G-SIBs). Third, \emph{the securities-loss channel does not survive the
horse race on conventional terms}: scaled unrealized losses are statistically insignificant
($t=-1.16$) once we condition on uninsured funding and size, whether we use total
(HTM$+$AFS) or HTM-only losses. The market priced the run, not---or at least far less---the
mark-to-market hole.

A cross-sectional event study with 165 banks invites two natural objections, and the
contribution of this paper beyond the basic horse race is to confront both head-on with
design-based methods rather than dismiss them. The first objection is that abnormal returns
are cross-sectionally correlated within a single crisis week, so heteroskedasticity-robust
$t$-statistics may overstate significance \citep{kolari2010,young2019}. We therefore subject
the horse race to \emph{randomization inference}: we permute each exposure across banks
2{,}000 times, rebuild the regression, and compare the actual coefficient to the permutation
distribution (Table~\ref{tab:ri}, Figure~\ref{fig:ri}). The uninsured coefficient sits far
outside its permutation interval ($p<0.001$); the securities coefficient is only marginal
($p=0.04$). The second objection is that uninsured-heavy banks differ from other banks on
size and on their securities books, so a linear regression may be picking up those
correlated exposures. We therefore build a \emph{matching estimator}: we compare
high-uninsured to low-uninsured banks that have been nearest-neighbor matched on size and
on securities losses, an approach that does not impose the linear functional form
\citep{imbens2004}. The matched difference in CARs is $-5.4$ percentage points ($t=-2.43$),
essentially unchanged from the raw $-5.7$pp gap, while covariate balance improves sharply
(Table~\ref{tab:matched}). We add a \emph{pre-event placebo window}: in the quiet
$[-6,-2]$ window, before the SVB announcement, banks were already falling together (the
Silvergate wind-down was underway), yet the \emph{cross-sectional} uninsured gradient is
flat ($t=-0.37$; Table~\ref{tab:placebo}). The uninsured gradient is specific to the run
week, not a pre-existing spread.

Finally, we treat the fragile securities-loss result as a quantitative object rather than a
bare ``insignificant.'' A minimum-detectable-effect (MDE) analysis (Table~\ref{tab:power})
shows the design is well-powered for the run channel---the MDE at 80\% power is about the
size of the estimated run effect itself---but only moderately powered for the smaller
mark-to-market channel, whose MDE is somewhat larger than the run effect. The honest reading
is therefore not ``mark-to-market losses did not matter'' but ``the market priced funding
fragility unambiguously and mark-to-market losses at most weakly, and our design can bound
but not sharply estimate the latter.''

We make four contributions. First, relative to the theory of bank runs
\citep{diamond1983,goldstein2005}, we provide clean asset-pricing evidence that the market's
ex-ante measure of run vulnerability is the uninsured-deposit share. Second, relative to the
2023-fragility literature \citep{jiang2024,metrick2024,drechsler2023}, which documents that
uninsured deposits and unrealized losses \emph{jointly} predicted deposit outflows and
failure, we show that in the \emph{cross-section of equity returns} the two are not
co-equal: conditional on funding structure, mark-to-market losses add little. Third, we
demonstrate a template for making a small-sample cross-sectional event study credible
through matching, randomization inference, a placebo window, and an explicit power analysis.
Fourth, we are explicit about what a machine can and cannot do: three refinements that would
sharpen the test---hand-corrected multi-bank uninsured aggregation, a bank-specific-news
confound screen, and realized deposit outflows as a second outcome---are deliberately
reserved as the companion student contribution and are described in Section~\ref{sec:concl}.
Section~\ref{sec:bg} develops hypotheses, Section~\ref{sec:lit} places the paper in the
literature, Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and design, Section~\ref{sec:res}
presents the main results, Section~\ref{sec:design} the design-based inference,
Section~\ref{sec:het} heterogeneity, Section~\ref{sec:power} the power analysis,
Section~\ref{sec:robust} additional robustness, and Section~\ref{sec:concl} concludes.

\section{Institutional Background and Hypotheses}\label{sec:bg}
\paragraph{The episode.} On the afternoon of Wednesday, March 8, 2023, SVB announced that it
had sold essentially its entire \$21 billion available-for-sale securities portfolio at a
loss and would attempt a \$2.25 billion capital raise. The disclosure crystallized a simple
arithmetic that applied, in varying degrees, to the whole industry: a bank that had funded
long-duration securities with deposits during the zero-rate era now held those securities at
a deep unrealized loss, and if enough depositors withdrew, the bank would be forced to
realize the loss and could fail. Over March 9--10 SVB's uninsured depositors withdrew at
scale; the FDIC took the bank into receivership on Friday, March 10; and contagion spread to
Signature Bank (closed March 12) and other regional banks through Monday, March 13
\citep{metrick2024}. We take day 0 to be March 8 (the announcement, after the close) and
cumulate abnormal returns through March 13.

\paragraph{Two exposures, two hypotheses.} A bank financed by uninsured deposits faces a
coordination problem: because uninsured claims are not protected, depositors have an
incentive to withdraw first if they fear others will \citep{diamond1983,goldstein2005}.
Equity holders, anticipating fire-sale liquidation and franchise destruction, should mark
down banks with more uninsured funding when run risk becomes salient. This motivates the
run-channel hypothesis:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{H1 (Run channel).} CARs in the SVB window are decreasing in the 2022Q4
uninsured-deposit ratio.
\end{quote}
Alternatively, if the market's concern is economic insolvency from interest-rate risk, then
banks with larger unrealized securities losses---HTM losses in particular, which are not
marked through regulatory capital---should fall most. This motivates the competing
mark-to-market hypothesis:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{H2 (Mark-to-market channel).} CARs are decreasing in unrealized securities losses
scaled by equity.
\end{quote}
H1 and H2 are not mutually exclusive---the theory of \citet{jiang2024} is precisely that
they interact---but the \emph{conditional} horse race, which exposure retains explanatory
power when both are included, discriminates between them. The central empirical question is
which exposure the equity market treated as the binding constraint in the days when the run
was actually happening.

\paragraph{Why the distinction matters for policy.} The two hypotheses point to different
regulatory fixes. If the mark-to-market channel dominates, the remedy is accounting and
capital treatment of the securities book---for example, flowing AFS and HTM unrealized
losses through regulatory capital, or restricting the reclassification of securities into
HTM \citep{granja2023}. If the run channel dominates, the remedy is on the liability
side---deposit-insurance design, limits on uninsured-funding concentration, and liquidity
requirements calibrated to run-off speed \citep{drechsler2023}. Distinguishing the two in the
cross-section of market reactions is thus directly policy-relevant, and it is the object of
this paper.

\section{Related Literature}\label{sec:lit}
This paper connects three strands. The first is the theory and measurement of \emph{bank
runs and uninsured funding}. \citet{diamond1983} formalized the maturity-transformation
fragility that makes demandable deposits run-prone, and \citet{goldstein2005} sharpened the
comparative statics of the run probability in a global-games setting. Empirically,
\citet{egan2017} estimate a structural model of deposit competition in which uninsured
deposits are the fragile margin, and \citet{drechsler2021} show that the deposit franchise
insulates banks from rate risk precisely because insured, sticky deposits are rate-insensitive
---which by contradistinction identifies uninsured deposits as the exposed liability.
\citet{drechsler2023} return to this margin directly after 2023, arguing that uninsured
deposits are the locus of banking-system fragility. Our contribution to this strand is to
show that the equity market's ex-ante pricing of run vulnerability loads on exactly the
uninsured-deposit share this literature identifies as fragile.

The second strand is the fast-growing \emph{2023 banking-crisis literature}. \citet{jiang2024}
document that the marked-to-market value of U.S.\ bank assets fell sharply during the 2022
tightening and that banks with large unrealized losses \emph{and} large uninsured-deposit
funding were jointly the most exposed to a run and to insolvency; \citet{metrick2024}
provides a narrative and taxonomy of the panic; and \citet{granja2023} studies the strategic
reclassification of securities into HTM that let banks avoid marking losses through capital.
A parallel literature documents the mechanics of the run itself: \citet{choi2023} study
contagion across banks, and \citet{cookson2023} show that social media accelerated the
deposit flight. Where this literature typically treats unrealized losses and uninsured
deposits as \emph{joint} predictors of failure and outflows, we ask the sharper
cross-sectional question---conditional on funding structure, does the mark-to-market hole add
explanatory power for \emph{equity returns}?---and find that it does not, or does so only
weakly.

The third strand is \emph{event-study and design-based inference methodology}.
\citet{mackinlay1997} is the canonical reference for the market-model event study we use.
A recurring concern in single-event studies is that abnormal returns are cross-sectionally
correlated, which invalidates the usual independence assumptions and inflates test statistics
\citep{kolari2010}; \citet{young2019} shows more broadly that randomization (Fisherian)
inference frequently overturns significance that conventional standard errors report. We use
these tools not as decoration but as the core of our identification: the run channel survives
randomization inference and a matching estimator \citep{imbens2004}, while the mark-to-market
channel is fragile to them. Finally, on the interpretation of the weaker channel, we follow
\citet{abadie2020}, who argues that a statistically insignificant estimate should be read
through its confidence interval and power rather than treated as proof of no effect; our
minimum-detectable-effect analysis states the mark-to-market result as a bound rather than a
bare null.

\section{Data and Research Design}\label{sec:data}
\paragraph{Sample.} We start from all common shares (CRSP share codes 10, 11) on the NYSE,
AMEX, or Nasdaq with an SIC code in the depository/holding-bank range (6020--6079, 6712)
active in the SVB window, yielding 246 bank stocks with computable CARs. We merge each to its
regulatory filer and require 2022Q4 balance-sheet data and a valid estimation window, leaving
165 banks; the uninsured-deposit ratio is available for 157 of them. The sample is
overwhelmingly bank and thrift holding companies (142 bank holding companies, 15 thrift
holding companies, and 8 standalone banks or non-member institutions).

\paragraph{Balance-sheet exposures.} Bank fundamentals are filed at two levels, and getting
the aggregation right is central to the exercise. Assets, equity, and securities are reported
on the \emph{consolidated} FR Y-9C at the holding-company level; we take total assets
(BHCK2170), equity (BHCK3210), HTM amortized cost/fair value (BHCK1754/1771), and AFS
amortized cost/fair value (BHCK1772/1773), and compute the \emph{securities unrealized loss}
as (amortized cost $-$ fair value), scaled by equity. Summing subsidiary Call Reports would
double-count multi-tier holding companies---for example, aggregating subsidiaries pushes New
York Community Bancorp to \$270B versus its true consolidated \$90.1B---so we do \emph{not}
sum for the consolidated quantities. Estimated uninsured deposits (RCON5597) are reported
only in bank-level Call Reports, so for the baseline we take the holding company's
\emph{lead} (largest-asset) subsidiary bank. Correctly aggregating uninsured deposits across
the subsidiaries of multi-bank holding companies is a hand-verification task reserved for the
companion student contribution (Section~\ref{sec:concl}); it is deliberately not attempted
here. The \emph{uninsured-deposit ratio} is RCON5597 divided by consolidated assets, and
\emph{size} is $\ln(\text{assets})$.

\paragraph{Event study.} Day 0 is 2023-03-08. We estimate a market model
$R_{it}=\alpha_i+\beta_i R_{mt}+\varepsilon_{it}$ over the $[-252,-46]$ trading-day window
against the CRSP value-weighted index, and cumulate abnormal returns
$\text{CAR}_i=\sum_{t\in W}(R_{it}-\hat\alpha_i-\hat\beta_i R_{mt})$ over event windows $W$
\citep{mackinlay1997}. Our primary window is $[0,+3]$ (March 8 close through March 13); we
report $[0,+1]$, $[0,+5]$, $[-1,+3]$, and a pre-event placebo window $[-6,-2]$ as
robustness. Predictors are winsorized at 1/99 and standardized, so a coefficient reads as
the effect of a one-standard-deviation increase in CAR points. Standard errors are
heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1), and Section~\ref{sec:design} supplements them with
design-based inference.

\paragraph{Specification.} The core cross-sectional regression is
\begin{equation}
\text{CAR}_i \;=\; \gamma_0 + \gamma_1\,\widetilde{\text{Uninsured}}_i
+ \gamma_2\,\widetilde{\text{SecLoss}}_i + \gamma_3\,\widetilde{\text{Size}}_i
+ \varepsilon_i,
\label{eq:xsec}
\end{equation}
where tildes denote winsorized, standardized exposures. H1 predicts $\gamma_1<0$ and H2
predicts $\gamma_2<0$; the horse race asks which survives when both are present.
Table~\ref{tab:sumstats} reports summary statistics and Table~\ref{tab:corr} the correlation
matrix. The median bank has a 32\% uninsured-deposit ratio and securities losses of about
22\% of equity; uninsured exposure and securities losses are only modestly correlated
($\rho=0.23$), so the horse race is not undermined by collinearity, and matching on the
securities book while comparing on uninsured exposure has adequate common support.

\section{Main Results}\label{sec:res}
\paragraph{The shock.} Table~\ref{tab:cars} shows the window was large and negative across
banks: a mean CAR of $-4.6\%$ over $[0,+1]$, $-14.1\%$ over $[0,+3]$, and $-15.3\%$ over
$[-1,+3]$, all with $t<-12$. The $[-6,-2]$ placebo window is $-2.7\%$---non-trivial, because
the Silvergate wind-down was unfolding that week---but roughly one-fifth the event-window
loss. The industry-wide fall is the backdrop; the cross-section is where the two hypotheses
are tested.

\paragraph{The horse race.} Table~\ref{tab:main} is the core result. In column (1),
uninsured exposure alone explains 14\% of the cross-sectional variation in CARs, with a
one-SD effect of $-5.0$pp ($t=-2.55$). Securities losses alone (column 2) are insignificant
($t=-1.57$, $R^2=0.06$). With both included (column 3) the uninsured coefficient
strengthens ($-4.6$pp, $t=-2.92$) while securities losses fall to $t=-1.09$. Adding size
(column 4) leaves uninsured at $-4.2$pp ($t=-3.05$); excluding G-SIBs (column 5) gives
$-4.1$pp ($t=-3.09$) and the highest fit ($R^2=0.21$). H1 is supported throughout; H2 is
not supported on conventional terms once we condition on funding and size.
Figure~\ref{fig:bin} shows the pattern is monotone: banks in the top uninsured-exposure
deciles fell roughly twice as far as those in the bottom, and Figure~\ref{fig:quart} shows
the same gradient across quartiles. The point estimate on securities losses is consistently
negative and about half the size of the uninsured coefficient; we return in
Section~\ref{sec:power} to what the design can and cannot say about a channel of that
magnitude.

\section{Design-Based Inference: Matching and Randomization}\label{sec:design}
A cross-sectional event study of 165 banks in a single crisis week is exactly the setting in
which robust $t$-statistics can mislead---abnormal returns are cross-sectionally correlated
\citep{kolari2010}, and the exposures of interest are correlated with size and with each
other. We therefore stress the horse race with three checks that do not rely on the
parametric standard error.

\paragraph{Matching estimator.} If the uninsured effect merely reflects the fact that
high-uninsured banks are larger or hold worse securities books, then comparing high- and
low-uninsured banks with those covariates balanced should erase it. We split banks at the
median uninsured ratio and nearest-neighbor match each high-uninsured bank to a low-uninsured
bank on standardized size and securities losses (1:1, without replacement, 0.5-SD caliper),
yielding 54 matched pairs \citep{imbens2004}. Table~\ref{tab:matched} reports the results.
The raw high$-$low difference in CAR$[0,+3]$ is $-5.7$pp ($t=-2.74$); after matching, which
cuts the size gap from 0.36 to 0.04 standardized units and the securities-loss gap from 0.33
to 0.06, the difference is $-5.4$pp ($t=-2.43$). The run effect is a comparison of otherwise
similar banks that differ in their uninsured funding, not an artifact of size or of the
securities book. Reassuringly, the same matched pairs show \emph{no} difference in the
pre-event placebo window ($+0.4$pp, $t=+0.97$): matched high- and low-uninsured banks moved
together \emph{before} the run and diverged only during it.

\paragraph{Randomization inference.} Table~\ref{tab:ri} and Figure~\ref{fig:ri} report
design-based inference from 2{,}000 permutations of each exposure across banks. We permute
the (predetermined) uninsured ratio across banks, refit equation~\eqref{eq:xsec}, and
collect the coefficient; the randomization $p$-value is the share of permutations whose
coefficient is at least as extreme as the actual one. For the uninsured channel the actual
coefficient ($-0.042$) lies far outside the permutation 95\% interval
($[-0.020,+0.020]$), giving $p<0.001$: the run result is not an artifact of cross-sectional
correlation or of HC1 asymptotics. For the securities channel the actual coefficient
($-0.020$) sits essentially at the edge of its permutation interval, $p=0.04$---marginal, and
notably weaker than the run channel by any design-based reading. The securities channel is the
one place where randomization inference is \emph{less} conservative than the robust standard
error: its HC1 standard error ($0.018$) is $1.8$ times the permutation standard deviation
($0.010$). This gap is not collinearity---uninsured and securities losses are only weakly
correlated ($\rho=0.23$)---but heteroskedasticity: bank CARs are enormously dispersed (SD
$13\%$), so HC1 inflates the standard error to absorb that dispersion while the permutation
null assumes exchangeability and is therefore tighter. We accordingly treat HC1 as the
conservative, preferred inference for the mark-to-market channel and read its randomization
$p$-value as an upper bound on that channel's significance, not as evidence of an effect.
Randomization inference thus corroborates the asymmetry the horse race reports: the run channel
is unambiguous under both methods; the mark-to-market channel is, at best, weak.

\paragraph{Placebo window.} Table~\ref{tab:placebo} runs equation~\eqref{eq:xsec} with the
pre-event $[-6,-2]$ CAR as the dependent variable. Even though banks were already falling in
that window (the placebo mean CAR is $-2.7\%$; the Silvergate wind-down had begun), the
\emph{cross-sectional} uninsured gradient is flat and insignificant ($t=-0.37$), as are the
securities-loss and size gradients. Banks fell \emph{together} before the SVB announcement,
regardless of their uninsured funding; the uninsured gradient appears only in the run week.
This is a clean falsification: the run channel is specific to the event, not a pre-existing
spread that our window mechanically captures.

\section{Heterogeneity}\label{sec:het}
If the run channel is the market pricing genuine run vulnerability, it should be present
across observable subgroups and, plausibly, sharper where the run mechanism is more
dangerous. Table~\ref{tab:hetero} interacts both channels with four observable 2022Q4
moderators---above-median size, above-median securities losses, above-median HTM losses, and
a thrift-holding-company indicator---reporting the base (low-moderator) coefficient and the
interaction for each channel. Three patterns stand out and all point the same way. First, the
run channel is present in every subgroup: the base uninsured coefficient is negative and
significant across the board. Second, the run channel is \emph{amplified} exactly where the
mark-to-market hole is deepest---for banks with above-median HTM losses the uninsured
interaction is an additional $-6.9$pp ($t=-2.40$)---consistent with the theory of
\citet{jiang2024} that uninsured funding is most dangerous when it sits atop a large
unrealized loss, so that a run forces the loss to be realized. Third, the effect is not a
thrift-versus-bank artifact: the thrift interaction is essentially zero ($+0.6$pp,
$t=+0.31$). Crucially, these moderators are all observable balance-sheet characteristics; we
do \emph{not} interact the run channel with hand-collected variables such as
bank-specific-news exposure or realized deposit outflows, which are reserved for the student
contribution and are chosen precisely to be orthogonal to that reserved test.

\section{Is the Mark-to-Market Null Informative? Power and Minimum Detectable Effects}\label{sec:power}
The honest question raised by the horse race is whether the weak securities-loss result means
``mark-to-market losses did not matter'' or merely ``our design could not detect a
mark-to-market effect.'' Table~\ref{tab:power} answers it directly. For each predictor we
report the minimum detectable effect at 80\% power,
$\text{MDE}=(z_{0.975}+z_{0.80})\,\widehat{\text{SE}}\approx 2.80\,\widehat{\text{SE}}$,
expressed in CAR points, as a share of the cross-firm CAR standard deviation, and relative to
the estimated run effect. The design is well-powered for the run channel: its MDE is $0.038$,
about 92\% of the estimated run effect, so an effect that size was very likely to be
detected---and was. For the securities channel the MDE is $0.049$, about $1.18$ times the run
effect. The implication is twofold and stated as a bound, following \citet{abadie2020}. On
the one hand, had the market priced mark-to-market losses \emph{as strongly as} it priced
funding fragility, our design would probably have detected it; it did not, so a mark-to-market
channel of run-channel magnitude is ruled out. On the other hand, the design is only
moderately powered for a \emph{smaller} securities effect, so we cannot rule out a modest
mark-to-market contribution of roughly half the run-channel size---which is, in fact, the
point estimate we obtain. The null is therefore informative about the \emph{relative}
importance of the two channels, and we are careful not to overclaim that mark-to-market
losses were priced at exactly zero.

\section{Additional Robustness}\label{sec:robust}
Table~\ref{tab:robust} collects three further specifications. First, replacing total
securities losses with \emph{HTM-only} losses---the losses that are hidden from regulatory
capital and thus the sharpest test of the mark-to-market story---leaves the uninsured channel
at $-4.0$pp ($t=-3.13$) and the HTM channel insignificant ($t=-1.48$). Second and third,
using CAR$[0,+1]$ or CAR$[-1,+3]$ as the dependent variable preserves the uninsured effect
($t=-2.06$ and $t=-3.11$ respectively) while securities losses remain insignificant. The run
result is not an artifact of the securities-loss definition or of the choice of event window.
Column~(5) of Table~\ref{tab:main} already established that dropping the eight G-SIBs, if
anything, strengthens the run channel, so the result is not driven by the largest banks.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}
Across publicly-traded U.S.\ banks, the March 2023 equity collapse loaded on
uninsured-deposit funding fragility far more than on hidden securities losses, once size is
held fixed. The run channel is large ($-4.2$pp per SD), monotone, and robust to every design
we impose: a covariate-balanced matching estimator, randomization inference on 2{,}000
permutations, a clean pre-event placebo window, and G-SIB exclusion. The mark-to-market
channel is, by contrast, fragile: insignificant under robust inference, only marginal under
randomization inference, and about half the magnitude of the run channel---and a power
analysis shows our design can rule out a mark-to-market effect as large as the run channel
but not a modest one. The market behaved, in the crisis week, primarily as a run-risk pricer.
The policy implication mirrors the subsequent regulatory debate: deposit-insurance design and
uninsured-funding concentration, more than mark-to-market accounting of the securities book,
are what the equity market treated as making some banks fragile.

We are explicit about three limitations, each of which is the object of a reserved
hand-collection task in the companion student contribution, and none of which we attempt with
the automated data here. First, our baseline uses the \emph{lead} subsidiary bank's uninsured
deposits; correctly aggregating RCON5597 across the subsidiaries of multi-bank holding
companies, read by hand from each subsidiary's Call Report, would sharpen the key regressor
and is the first reserved task. Second, some banks had \emph{bank-specific} news during
March 8--13 (a capital raise, a downgrade, an idiosyncratic deposit-flight report) that could
confound the market-model abnormal return; hand-building a news-flag screen and re-running with
confounded banks excluded is the second reserved task. Third, the ultimate test of the run
hypothesis is whether uninsured exposure predicted \emph{realized} deposit outflows, not only
stock reactions; hand-coding actual Q1-2023 deposit runs from 10-Qs and press coverage, as a
second outcome, is the third reserved task. Each is a genuine research contribution that turns
this observable-data horse race into a sharper, hand-verified test, and each is deliberately
left for the student rather than approximated here. Extending the design to the full
2022--2023 tightening path is left to future work.
