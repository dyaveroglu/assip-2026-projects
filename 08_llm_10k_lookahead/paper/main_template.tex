\section{Introduction}
Large language models (LLMs) are being used to read corporate disclosures and produce
signals that predict stock returns \citep{lopezlira2023,kim2024,jha2024}. A natural worry
haunts every such exercise: when an LLM ``reads'' a 2019 annual report and rates its risk,
is it processing the \emph{text}, or is it recalling---from its training data---what
happened to that \emph{stock} afterward? Because a model like gpt-4o was trained on text
scraped through October 2023, its parameters may encode both the filing itself and the
subsequent price path. A return-predictability backtest that scores pre-training filings
then ``predicts'' their already-realized returns is not forecasting; it is
\emph{look-ahead contamination} \citep{glasserman2023,sarkar2024}. This paper asks how much
of the return-predictive power of an LLM risk score is real reading versus remembered
outcomes, and it proposes a simple, auditable test to tell them apart.

Our identification is a placebo built from two orthogonal manipulations. First, we score
each 10-K risk-factor section twice with the \emph{same} model and the \emph{same} prompt:
once on the \textbf{raw} text and once on an \textbf{anonymized} copy in which the firm's
name, ticker, dates, and state are redacted. If the model is genuinely reading risk, masking
identity should barely move the score or its return-predictive power; if it is recognizing
the firm, anonymization should strip predictive power away. Second, we split filings by the
model's training cutoff. \textbf{Pre-cutoff} filings (filed 2019--2021) have a 12-month
post-filing return window that closed \emph{before} October 2023, so the outcome could sit
in the model's training data---look-ahead is \emph{possible}. \textbf{Post-cutoff} filings
(filed in 2024) have both the text and the outcome \emph{after} the cutoff, so look-ahead is
\emph{impossible} by construction. The contamination signature is sharp and falsifiable: raw
scores should predict returns better than anonymized scores \emph{only} in the pre-cutoff
window, and that raw-versus-anon gap should vanish post-cutoff.

We contribute to three literatures. First, to the fast-growing literature applying LLMs to
finance \citep{lopezlira2023,kim2024,jha2024,kelly2019,hansen2023}, we add a diagnostic that
any such study can run before trusting a backtest. Second, to work on textual disclosure and
returns \citep{cohen2020,loughran2011,campbell2014,kravet2013,tetlock2007}, we bring a
design that separates information in the text from information about the firm. Third, to the
machine-learning literature on training-data memorization and benchmark contamination
\citep{carlini2021,sainz2023,magar2022,glasserman2023}, we provide a finance-specific,
economically interpretable measurement. Section~\ref{sec:hyp} develops hypotheses;
Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and the anonymization; Section~\ref{sec:res}
presents results; Section~\ref{sec:rob} reports robustness; Section~\ref{sec:concl}
concludes.

\section{Hypotheses}\label{sec:hyp}
Let $s^{\text{raw}}_i$ and $s^{\text{anon}}_i$ be the gpt-4o downside-risk score
(0--100, higher = worse expected 12-month outlook) for filing $i$ on the raw and anonymized
text, and let $R_i$ be the firm's 12-month post-filing buy-and-hold abnormal return (BHAR).

If the score reflects genuine reading of risk content, then anonymizing identity---which
leaves the risk content intact---should not change what the score knows:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{H1 (Reading).} The return-predictive slope of $s^{\text{raw}}$ equals that of
$s^{\text{anon}}$, in both the pre- and post-cutoff windows.
\end{quote}
If instead the score partly reflects recognition of the firm and recall of its realized
path, that channel is available only when (i) identity is visible and (ii) the outcome
predates the training cutoff:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{H2 (Remembering / look-ahead).} $s^{\text{raw}}$ predicts $R$ more strongly
(more negative slope) than $s^{\text{anon}}$ \emph{in the pre-cutoff window}, and the
raw-minus-anon predictive gap is larger pre-cutoff than post-cutoff.
\end{quote}
H1 and H2 make opposite predictions about a single object---the raw-versus-anon predictive
gap and its interaction with the training cutoff (the triple interaction
$s\times\text{raw}\times\text{post}$). A closely related implication concerns the
\emph{score gap} $g_i=s^{\text{raw}}_i-s^{\text{anon}}_i$ itself: under H2 the
identity-driven adjustment $g_i$ should be return-relevant pre-cutoff (the model nudges the
score toward the outcome it remembers) but not post-cutoff.
\begin{quote}
\textbf{H3 (Gap).} $g_i$ predicts $R_i$ in the pre-cutoff window but not post-cutoff.
\end{quote}

\section{Data and Variables}\label{sec:data}
\paragraph{Filings.} We draw 10-K risk-factor sections (Item 1A) from a reusable archive of
SEC EDGAR filings for CRSP/Compustat firms, which pre-extracts each item as plain text. We
keep genuine 10-Ks (not amendments) that contain a non-empty Item 1A, and form two windows
around gpt-4o's October-2023 knowledge cutoff: a \textbf{pre-cutoff} window of filings filed
2019--2021 and a \textbf{post-cutoff} window of filings filed in 2024. Within each window we
take one filing per firm (the latest in-window) and draw a random sample with a fixed seed.

\paragraph{Returns.} Each filing is linked to CRSP via the Compustat CIK and the CRSP-Compustat
link. Using CRSP monthly returns, we compute the buy-and-hold return over the $K$ calendar
months after the filing month ($K=3,6,12$) and subtract the compounded market return
(Fama--French market factor plus the risk-free rate) over the same months to form the
market-adjusted BHAR. The 12-month BHAR is the primary outcome; both windows have full
12-month return coverage (CRSP monthly extends through December 2025). The final sample keeps
filings with a valid 12-month BHAR.

\paragraph{LLM risk score.} We score each filing with gpt-4o (deployment
gpt-4o-2024-11-20; temperature 0), using one fixed prompt that asks for an integer 0--100
rating of downside risk to the stock over the next 12 months, given only the risk-factor
text (truncated to a fixed length so raw and anonymized inputs are comparable). Each filing
is scored twice---raw and anonymized---and we record both.

\paragraph{Anonymization.} The automated anonymizer redacts the firm's name and its
distinctive name-tokens ($\rightarrow$\texttt{[COMPANY]}), its CRSP ticker
($\rightarrow$\texttt{[TICKER]}), every four-digit year ($\rightarrow$\texttt{[YEAR]}), and
U.S. state names ($\rightarrow$\texttt{[STATE]}). This is a deliberate \emph{floor}: it
removes explicit identifiers but not paraphrased self-descriptions or brand and product
names, which a human can catch. Building that gold standard by hand---and measuring how much
identity still leaks through the automated version---is the manual task documented in
\texttt{STUDENT\_TASKS.md}; it is the natural next tightening of the placebo.

\paragraph{Sample.} Table~\ref{tab:sumstats} reports summary statistics for the scored
sample. Standardized scores (z-scored on the pooled raw$+$anon distribution) let the raw and
anonymized slopes be read on a common scale.
