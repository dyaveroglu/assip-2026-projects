\begin{abstract}
\noindent The years 2022--2025 delivered an unusually dense sequence of \emph{dated} U.S. policy
and technology shocks---from the CHIPS Act and ChatGPT to Silicon Valley Bank, the 2025
reciprocal tariffs, and the Reg-NMS half-cent tick. We assemble them into a single, uniformly
measured event-study atlas across about 2{,}000 large firms and ask how the shocks differ along
three axes: how much the market \emph{moves}, how much it \emph{differentiates} firms, and how it
\emph{propagates}. Three findings survive formal testing. First, cross-sectional differentiation
is \emph{real}: for the tariff shock, the Reg-NMS tick, and the SVB collapse, the event-window
dispersion of firm CARs exceeds a placebo distribution of pseudo-event dispersions (ratios
$1.5$--$1.9$, empirical $p<0.05$), and shock rankings by dispersion are stable across windows and
dispersion measures (Spearman $0.72$--$0.83$). Second, this differentiation is overwhelmingly
\emph{idiosyncratic}: market beta explains at most $14\%$ of the cross-sectional CAR variance and
usually far less, so the dispersion is firm-specific repricing---exactly what a firm-level
exposure measure should capture. A pooled firm$\times$shock regression on observable traits (size,
beta, idiosyncratic volatility) explains $21\%$ of $|\text{CAR}|$; the rest is the space reserved
for the cohort's hand-collected exposure test. Third, we are honest about what the small
cross-section of shocks cannot support: the widely quoted claim that differentiation ``scales with
shock size'' rests on a correlation of $0.69$ whose bootstrap $95\%$ interval is $[-0.04,0.97]$
and which falls to $0.26$ when the tariff shock is dropped---with $n=10$ shocks the design can
only reliably detect correlations above $0.78$, so we report this as suggestive, not established.
Propagation is heterogeneous ($I^2=99\%$ across shocks; SVB the negative-drift outlier), but a
high-powered pooled test detects modest post-event \emph{continuation} (within-shock, firm-clustered
top-minus-bottom announcement-quintile drift $+0.73\%$, $t=4.0$). The atlas is a reusable scaffold; its completion
is each student's exposure measure.\\[4pt]
\noindent\textbf{JEL:} G14, G18, E44. \quad
\textbf{Keywords:} event study, market efficiency, policy shocks, cross-sectional dispersion,
randomization inference, meta-analysis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Empirical finance studies shocks one at a time. Yet 2022--2025 handed markets a whole
\emph{sequence} of clean, dated shocks, and studying them together---on identical footing---asks a
question no single event study can: \emph{how do policy shocks differ}? This cohort capstone builds
that comparison. Using the shared market-model machinery behind the individual papers in this
program \citep{fama1970,mackinlay1997,brown1985,campbell1997}, we compute cumulative abnormal
returns (CARs) for a common sample of about 2{,}000 large firms around ten dated shocks
(Table~\ref{tab:atlas}) and characterize each along three axes: the aggregate \emph{level} move,
the cross-sectional \emph{differentiation} of firms, and the \emph{propagation} of the reaction
over the following days.

The first axis is the level move---how much the market index shifted. These range from a $+3.1\%$
pop on ChatGPT to a $-11.0\%$ two-day crash on the April-2025 reciprocal tariffs. The second, and
the heart of the paper, is \emph{differentiation}: how much firms diverged, measured by the
cross-sectional standard deviation (SD) of announcement CARs. Because a market-wide shock is
absorbed by the benchmark, the mean abnormal return is near zero by construction; the informative
object is the \emph{dispersion}, which measures how sharply a shock sorted winners from losers
\citep{roll1988,morck2000}. The third axis is \emph{propagation}: does the reaction complete
quickly, drift, or reverse \citep{bernard1989,debondt1985}?

A descriptive atlas is only a starting point; the contribution of this draft is to subject each
axis to a formal, design-based test, and to be candid about which claims the ten-shock
cross-section can and cannot bear. We add ten tests to the original three-table draft.

\textbf{Differentiation is real, and it is idiosyncratic.} A shock that genuinely sorts firms
should produce cross-sectional dispersion larger than what the same firms show on ordinary days.
We build a placebo engine: one market model per firm over the full sample, then the cross-sectional
SD of two-day CARs at the true event date compared against a distribution of pseudo-event dates
drawn away from all ten shocks. For the tariff shock, the Reg-NMS half-cent tick, and the SVB
collapse, the event dispersion is $1.5$--$1.9$ times the placebo mean and lands in the top few
percent of the placebo distribution ($p<0.05$; Table~\ref{tab:diff}, Figure~\ref{fig:placebo}).
The ranking of shocks by dispersion is not an artifact of the window or the statistic: it is stable
across the $[0,+1]$, $[0,+5]$, and $[-5,+10]$ windows and across SD, interquartile range, and
median absolute deviation (Spearman $0.72$--$0.83$, all $p<0.02$; Table~\ref{tab:rankcorr}).
Crucially, the differentiation is not a beta story: a firm-by-firm regression of announcement CARs
on market beta explains at most $14\%$ of the cross-sectional variance (for the tariff) and
essentially nothing for most shocks, leaving $92$--$100\%$ of the dispersion idiosyncratic
(Table~\ref{tab:beta}). This is the empirical signature that firm-specific \emph{exposure}, not
systematic risk, drives the sorting---which is precisely the variable the cohort reserves for
hand-collection.

\textbf{Observable traits explain part of it; exposure is the reserved next step.} Pooling all
$19{,}256$ firm$\times$shock observations, we regress $|\text{CAR}[0,+1]|$ on observable firm
characteristics---log market capitalization, market beta, and idiosyncratic volatility---with shock
and industry fixed effects and standard errors clustered by firm (Table~\ref{tab:pooled}). All three
enter positively and idiosyncratic volatility dominates ($t=11.3$); together with the fixed effects
they explain $21\%$ of the variation in reaction magnitude. The remaining variation is the space the
design deliberately leaves open: whether each shock's \emph{specific} firm-level exposure (import-input
intensity for tariffs, uninsured-deposit ratios for SVB, AI-exposure for the language-model shocks)
explains the residual dispersion, and whether that exposure was priced \emph{before} the
announcement. That test requires the exposure crosswalk the cohort is assembling, and we do not
attempt it with the generic observables here.

\textbf{We are honest about the small cross-section.} The draft's headline was that differentiation
``scales with shock size,'' summarized by a $0.69$ correlation between the absolute market move and
the cross-sectional dispersion. With only ten shocks this is fragile. A bootstrap gives a $95\%$
interval of $[-0.04,0.97]$; leave-one-shock-out drops the correlation to $0.26$ when the tariff
shock is removed (Table~\ref{tab:boot}, Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap}); and a power calculation shows
that with $n=10$ the design can only reliably detect correlations above $0.78$
(Table~\ref{tab:power}). The Reg-NMS tick makes the point vivid: it differentiates firms as sharply
as the tariff (dispersion $5.1\%$) despite a tiny $-1.1\%$ market move. We therefore \emph{retract
the strong scaling claim} and report it as suggestive. What is robust is that differentiation is
real, idiosyncratic, and largest for the tariff, the tick, and SVB.

\textbf{Propagation is heterogeneous but shows modest continuation.} Post-event drift differs
enormously across shocks: a random-effects meta-analysis \citep{dersimonian1986,higgins2002} of
the mean drift returns a pooled estimate indistinguishable from zero but with $I^2=99\%$
(Table~\ref{tab:metare}, Figure~\ref{fig:forest})---these are genuinely different events, and SVB is
the negative-drift ($-3.5\%$) contagion outlier. Yet pooling firms and sorting by announcement CAR,
the highest-power test we run, reveals a monotone pattern: firms that fell most on impact keep
falling and firms that rose keep rising, a top-minus-bottom quintile drift of $+0.73\%$ ($t=3.9$;
Table~\ref{tab:overreact})---modest \emph{under-reaction}, not the over-reaction a contrarian would
predict.

We make four contributions. First, we provide a \emph{comparative} event-study lens on a dense
natural sequence of policy shocks, measured on strictly common footing. Second, we show how to make
a cross-shock atlas rigorous with design-based tools---placebo/permutation dispersion tests,
rank-stability checks, pooled clustered regressions, random-effects meta-analysis, bootstrap, and
minimum-detectable-effect calculations---rather than leaning on a handful of fragile cross-shock
correlations. Third, we correct an over-strong claim in the original draft and quantify exactly why
$n=10$ cannot support it. Fourth, we locate the differentiation in idiosyncratic, firm-specific
repricing, setting up the cohort's reserved exposure test as the natural completion. Section~2
describes the shock wave and hypotheses, Section~3 the literature, Section~4 the data and design,
Section~5 tests whether the differentiation is real, Section~6 decomposes it into systematic and
idiosyncratic parts, Section~7 confronts the fragility of the size-scaling claim, Section~8 studies
propagation, Section~9 reports power, and Section~10 concludes with the reserved exposure test.

\section{The Shock Wave and Hypotheses}\label{sec:bg}
\paragraph{Ten dated shocks.} We study ten dated events spanning industrial policy, tax, technology,
banking, disclosure, trade, crypto regulation, and market microstructure (Table~\ref{tab:atlas}):
the CHIPS Act signing (Aug 2022), the IRA buyback excise tax (Aug 2022), the ChatGPT release (Nov
2022) and GPT-4 release (Mar 2023), the Silicon Valley Bank collapse (Mar 2023), the SEC five-day
13D rule (Feb 2024), the April-2025 reciprocal tariff shock and the tariff pause days later, the
GENIUS Act (Jul 2025), and the Reg-NMS half-cent tick pilot (Nov 2025). Each is ``owned'' by the
cohort member whose individual project studies it; the ownership is what makes the reserved
exposure test feasible. We use a single calendar date per shock and are explicit
(Section~\ref{sec:concl}) that the exact intraday timing---announcement versus effective, pre-
versus post-close---is part of the reserved hand-collection and is not resolved here.

\paragraph{Three axes and hypotheses.} The atlas measures each shock on three axes. The
\emph{level} is the raw market move over $[0,+1]$. \emph{Differentiation} is the cross-sectional SD
of firm CARs over $[0,+1]$: a large, uniform shock moves every firm the same way (low dispersion),
while a shock that reallocates value across firms produces high dispersion. \emph{Propagation} is
captured by the mean drift over $[+2,+10]$ and by the cross-firm correlation between the
announcement CAR and the subsequent drift (positive $=$ continuation, negative $=$ reversal). These
motivate three hypotheses:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{H1 (Real differentiation).} Large policy shocks produce cross-sectional CAR dispersion
above the level firms exhibit on ordinary days.\\
\textbf{H2 (Idiosyncratic differentiation).} The dispersion is firm-specific, not a repricing of
systematic (beta) risk.\\
\textbf{H3 (Efficient propagation).} Post-event drift is small and mean-reverting, consistent with
prompt price discovery, absent a contagion mechanism.
\end{quote}
Our evidence supports H1 for the three largest shocks, supports H2 broadly, and gives a nuanced
verdict on H3: propagation is efficient on average but with a systematic component of modest
under-reaction and one clear contagion outlier (SVB).

\paragraph{The ten shocks, one by one.} Because each shock is owned by a cohort member, it is worth
walking the atlas across its three axes (all figures from Table~\ref{tab:atlas}). The \emph{CHIPS Act}
signing (owned by A.\ Tang) moved the market $+1.7\%$ with modest dispersion ($2.9\%$) and a small
negative drift. The \emph{IRA buyback excise tax} was nearly a non-event in aggregate ($-0.7\%$) with
the lowest dispersion of all ($2.5\%$). The two language-model shocks bracket the technology story:
\emph{ChatGPT} (N.\ Ngo) produced a $+3.1\%$ pop and $3.1\%$ dispersion, while \emph{GPT-4} moved the
index only $+1.0\%$ yet still dispersed firms ($3.2\%$). The \emph{SVB collapse} (A.\ Zhang) is the
propagation outlier: a $-3.7\%$ move, $4.2\%$ dispersion, and a large $-3.5\%$ drift with a positive
reversal correlation ($+0.42$)---firms that fell most kept falling, the contagion signature. The
\emph{SEC five-day 13D rule} was procedurally invisible ($-0.1\%$, $2.8\%$ dispersion). The
\emph{April-2025 reciprocal tariff} (D.\ Yaveroglu) is the largest shock on every axis: $-11.0\%$
market move and $5.8\%$ dispersion, its $+6.2\%$ \emph{pause} days later partly reversing it with a
strong positive drift ($+2.6\%$). The \emph{GENIUS Act} (D.\ Jo\'rabekova) barely moved the broad
index ($+0.1\%$) as a targeted crypto-regulatory event. Finally, the \emph{Reg-NMS half-cent tick}
pilot (K.\ Borra) is the atlas's most instructive case: a tiny $-1.1\%$ aggregate move but the
second-highest dispersion ($5.2\%$)---a microstructure rule that reshuffled firms without moving the
market, the sharpest counterexample to ``differentiation scales with size.''

\section{Related Literature}\label{sec:lit}
This paper connects four strands. The first is \emph{event-study methodology}. The market-model
CAR and its inference are standard \citep{brown1985,mackinlay1997,campbell1997}, but short-horizon
tests are vulnerable to event-induced variance and to cross-sectional correlation of abnormal
returns \citep{boehmer1991,kolari2010}. A market-wide policy shock is the extreme case: abnormal
returns are strongly cross-sectionally correlated and the event mechanically inflates variance.
This is exactly why we do not test mean CARs (which are near zero by construction here) but instead
test the \emph{dispersion} against a placebo distribution and pool firm$\times$shock reactions with
firm-clustered standard errors---design-based analogues of the corrections these papers propose.

The second strand is the \emph{cross-sectional dispersion of returns} as information. \citet{roll1988}
showed that firm-specific return variation is large and largely unexplained by systematic factors,
and \citet{morck2000} linked cross-firm return synchronicity to how much firm-specific information
is capitalized. Our differentiation axis is a policy-shock version of this idea: a shock that
capitalizes firm-specific information produces idiosyncratic dispersion, which is what
Table~\ref{tab:beta} finds. The reserved exposure test asks whether a \emph{named} exposure
accounts for that idiosyncratic dispersion.

The third strand is \emph{post-event drift and over/under-reaction}. \citet{bernard1989} document
post-earnings-announcement drift (under-reaction); \citet{debondt1985} document long-horizon
over-reaction and reversal. Our pooled quintile test (Table~\ref{tab:overreact}) is a short-horizon
policy-shock version: the modest continuation we find sits on the under-reaction side, consistent
with information diffusing over several days---most dramatically for the banking-contagion shock.

The fourth strand is \emph{evidence synthesis and design-based inference}. Aggregating estimates
across heterogeneous events is a meta-analysis problem; we use the DerSimonian--Laird random-effects
estimator \citep{dersimonian1986} and the $I^2$ heterogeneity statistic \citep{higgins2002} to
characterize---not to average away---the enormous cross-shock heterogeneity. For the small
cross-section of shocks we lean on resampling and randomization ideas \citep{efron1979,young2019}
and on the discipline of reporting a null through its power and confidence interval
\citep{abadie2020}, so that ``differentiation scales with size'' is stated as a bounded, falsifiable
claim rather than a single fragile correlation. The policy-shock setting itself is motivated by the
economic-policy-uncertainty literature \citep{baker2016}, which treats dated policy events as
first-order drivers of asset prices.

Methodologically, the paper's stance is that a \emph{comparative} atlas of shocks is worth building
only if it is held to the same inferential standard as a single event study---and that meeting that
standard requires importing tools finance rarely uses side by side. Placebo/permutation tests of
dispersion, rank-stability checks across estimators, random-effects synthesis with explicit
heterogeneity accounting, and minimum-detectable-effect reporting are each standard in some
discipline; combining them lets a ten-shock cross-section make honest claims without pretending to a
sample size it does not have. The alternative---reading a handful of cross-shock correlations as
facts---is precisely the practice this draft corrects. In that sense the paper is as much about
\emph{how} to compare shocks as about the shocks themselves.

\section{Data and Research Design}\label{sec:data}
\paragraph{Sample.} We take the daily returns of the roughly 2{,}000 largest U.S. firms by 2022
market capitalization from 2021-06 through 2025-12 (CRSP \texttt{dsf} and, for 2025,
\texttt{dsf\_v2}), benchmarked to the Fama-French market return (which extends through the 2025
events). Firm characteristics---market capitalization (for size), SIC industry, and market
beta---are observable and standard; we add each firm's idiosyncratic volatility, computed as the
standard deviation of its market-model residual over the full sample. None of these is a
shock-specific exposure measure. Table~\ref{tab:sumstats} summarizes the observable
characteristics: the sample averages a market beta near one and a log market capitalization of
about $9.3$, and the number of firms with a clean market model is stable at roughly $1{,}900$--$2{,}000$
across shocks, so the atlas compares the same broad universe at each event.

\paragraph{Firm-level CARs.} For each shock we estimate a market model
$R_{it}=\alpha_i+\beta_i R_{mt}+\varepsilon_{it}$ over the window $[-252,-46]$ trading days
relative to the event and cumulate abnormal returns over a pre-window $[-5,-1]$, an announcement
window $[0,+1]$, a week $[0,+5]$, a wide window $[-5,+10]$, and a drift window $[+2,+10]$. For each
shock we report the raw market move, the mean and cross-sectional SD of announcement CARs, the
pre-window and drift means, and the cross-firm reversal correlation between the announcement and
drift windows (Table~\ref{tab:atlas}). Because a market-wide shock is absorbed by the benchmark,
mean abnormal returns are near zero by construction; the informative statistics are the
cross-sectional \emph{dispersion} and the \emph{propagation} measures.

\paragraph{The placebo engine.} To test whether a shock's dispersion is genuinely elevated
(H1), we estimate one market model per firm over the full sample, form daily abnormal returns, and
compute the cross-sectional SD of two-day CARs at the true event date and at $600$ pseudo-event
dates drawn from the trading calendar but excluded from a $\pm 15$-day neighborhood of every real
shock. The empirical $p$-value is the share of pseudo-events whose dispersion equals or exceeds the
real one. Two caveats we state plainly: the full-sample $\alpha,\beta$ used for the placebo carry a
mild look-ahead (they use the whole period, not only pre-event data), and because 2022--2025 is a
dense shock window, some pseudo-event dates fall near \emph{other} shocks, which \emph{inflates} the
placebo distribution and makes the test conservative---so a rejection is if anything understated.

\paragraph{Pooled design and inference.} The atlas's ten cross-shock statistics are, by
construction, a sample of $n=10$: any correlation computed across shocks is underpowered and we
treat it as descriptive, guarding every such claim with a bootstrap and a minimum-detectable-effect
calculation. The statistical power of the paper lives instead in the pooled firm$\times$shock
tests, which use $\sim$19{,}000 observations. There, the same firm appears in up to ten shocks and
each shock is a common cross-sectional event, so residuals are correlated within firm \emph{and}
within shock; we absorb the shock (and industry) dimension with fixed effects and cluster standard
errors by firm ($\sim$1{,}989 clusters), rather than clustering on the ten shocks, which are far too
few for reliable cluster-robust inference.

\paragraph{The reserved boundary.} Everything in this paper uses observable data. The cohort's
reserved contribution---each shock's exact event time and its firm-level exposure measure, assembled
into an \texttt{exposure\_by\_shock.csv} crosswalk---is deliberately \emph{not} constructed here. The
central open questions (does named exposure explain the idiosyncratic dispersion? was exposure
priced before the announcement?) are left for that crosswalk; the size/beta/volatility observables
we use are chosen precisely because they are generic and orthogonal to the reserved measure.

\section{Is the Differentiation Real?}\label{sec:diff}
\paragraph{The atlas.} Table~\ref{tab:atlas} and Figure~\ref{fig:atlas} lay out the ten shocks. The
market moves span $-11.0\%$ (tariff) to $+6.1\%$ (tariff pause) and $+3.1\%$ (ChatGPT); the
cross-sectional dispersion of announcement CARs spans $2.5\%$ (IRA excise tax) to $5.8\%$ (tariff)
and $5.2\%$ (Reg-NMS tick). Mean announcement CARs hover near zero, as expected.

\paragraph{Dispersion exceeds placebo for the three largest sorters.} Table~\ref{tab:diff} and
Figure~\ref{fig:placebo} report the placebo test. The average firm shows a two-day dispersion of
about $2.8\%$ on ordinary days. Three shocks clear that bar decisively: the Reg-NMS half-cent tick
(dispersion $5.1\%$, ratio $1.85$, empirical $p=0.006$), the reciprocal tariff ($5.1\%$, ratio
$1.83$, $p=0.009$), and the SVB collapse ($4.1\%$, ratio $1.46$, $p=0.034$). The two language-model
releases and the tariff pause sit modestly above placebo (ratios $\approx 1.15$, $p\approx 0.20$),
and the procedural shocks (CHIPS, 13D rule, GENIUS Act, IRA excise) are indistinguishable from an
ordinary day. Differentiation, then, is real but concentrated: only the largest real and regulatory
reallocations sort firms beyond normal daily noise. This supports H1 for the three leading shocks
and, notably, shows a microstructure rule (the tick) differentiating as sharply as a macro trade
shock.

\paragraph{The ranking is robust to how dispersion is measured.} A skeptic might worry the ranking
depends on the two-day window or the SD statistic. It does not. Table~\ref{tab:rankcorr} shows that
the shock ranking by $[0,+1]$ SD correlates $0.72$ with the $[0,+5]$ ranking, $0.79$ with the
$[-5,+10]$ ranking, $0.81$ with the interquartile-range ranking, and $0.83$ with the median-absolute
-deviation ranking (all $p<0.02$). The differentiation ordering is a property of the shocks, not of
the measurement choice.

\section{Systematic versus Idiosyncratic Differentiation}\label{sec:beta}
\paragraph{Beta explains almost none of it.} If a shock simply re-priced systematic risk, firms
would sort by market beta and the dispersion would be a beta phenomenon. Table~\ref{tab:beta}
regresses each shock's firm CARs on market beta. Even for the tariff---where high-beta firms did
fall more (slope $+4.1$, $t=17.8$)---beta explains only $14\%$ of the cross-sectional variance;
for most shocks the $R^2$ is below $0.01$, and the idiosyncratic share of dispersion is $92$--$100\%$.
The differentiation is overwhelmingly firm-specific. This is the central bridge to the reserved
test: idiosyncratic dispersion is what a \emph{named} firm-level exposure is built to explain, and
its dominance here is why the exposure crosswalk is the natural completion of the atlas rather than
an afterthought.

\paragraph{Observable traits explain about a fifth.} Table~\ref{tab:pooled} pools all $19{,}256$
firm$\times$shock reactions and regresses $|\text{CAR}[0,+1]|$ on log market cap, market beta, and
idiosyncratic volatility, with shock and industry fixed effects and firm-clustered standard errors.
All three predictors enter positively: larger firms, higher-beta firms, and---most strongly---more
volatile firms react more (idiosyncratic volatility $t=11.3$). The model explains $21\%$ of the
variation in reaction magnitude. That a fifth is explained by generic observables, with four-fifths
left over, is the quantitative statement of how much room remains for the shock-specific exposure
measures the cohort will bring.

The three coefficients are also economically legible. The idiosyncratic-volatility loading
($+1.15$ percentage points of $|\text{CAR}|$ per point of firm volatility, $t=11.3$) says that
firms which are ordinarily more idiosyncratic move more on \emph{any} shock---a mechanical but
important control, because it is the part of the reaction that is \emph{not} about the shock's
content. The beta loading ($+0.18$, $t=2.5$) captures the systematic component the per-shock
regressions in Table~\ref{tab:beta} isolate. The size loading ($+0.05$, $t=2.4$) is small but
positive: among large caps, the very largest react slightly more, consistent with their heavier
index and macro exposure. Critically, once these generic channels and the shock and industry fixed
effects are absorbed, the residual is what a \emph{named} exposure should explain---and whether it
does is a test we deliberately leave open, because building the exposure regressor is the cohort's
reserved contribution, not ours to pre-empt with an observable proxy.

\section{Does Differentiation Scale with Size? A Fragility Analysis}\label{sec:fragile}
The original draft's headline was that differentiation scales with the aggregate move, a correlation
of $0.69$ between $|$market move$|$ and cross-sectional dispersion. Section~\ref{sec:diff} already
hinted at trouble---the Reg-NMS tick differentiates as much as the tariff with a tiny market
move---and a formal fragility analysis confirms it. Table~\ref{tab:boot} bootstraps the correlation
over the ten shocks: the point estimate is $0.686$, but the $95\%$ bootstrap interval is
$[-0.04,0.97]$, comfortably including zero (Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap}). Leave-one-shock-out is
equally telling: dropping the tariff shock alone collapses the correlation to $0.26$. The claim is
essentially one influential observation. Table~\ref{tab:power} makes the reason precise: with $n=10$
shocks, a design has $80\%$ power only for correlations above $0.78$; a true correlation of $0.69$
would fail to reach significance more often than not. We therefore \emph{retract the strong scaling
claim} and restate it as a suggestive pattern awaiting more shocks.

Grouping shocks into coarse families (Table~\ref{tab:typebucket}) is descriptive only---each family
has just two members---but it tells the same story without over-reaching: the trade family
(dispersion $4.7\%$) and the micro/disclosure family (driven by the tick, $4.0\%$) differentiate
most, while the industrial/tax family (procedural) differentiates least ($2.7\%$). Aggregate size is
one correlate of differentiation, but not the only one, and the ten-shock cross-section cannot
adjudicate which dominates.

\section{Propagation: Drift, Reversal, and Contagion}\label{sec:prop}
\paragraph{Drift is heterogeneous, not zero-on-average-because-efficient.} Table~\ref{tab:metare}
and Figure~\ref{fig:forest} report a random-effects meta-analysis of the mean $[+2,+10]$ drift
across shocks. The pooled estimate is $-0.01\%$ with a $95\%$ interval spanning
$[-0.93,+0.90]$---but this near-zero average masks extreme heterogeneity ($Q=947$ on 9 df, $I^2=99\%$).
The shocks are not drawing from a common drift; they are genuinely different events, and averaging
them is meaningless except to \emph{quantify} that heterogeneity, which is the honest use of the
$I^2$ statistic. The forest plot shows SVB as the clear negative-drift outlier ($-3.5\%$), with the
tariff pause on the positive side. The pre-window mean is slightly negative on average
($-0.89\%$, $[-1.47,-0.30]$), but again with $I^2=99\%$: we do not read it as clean anticipation,
because isolating leakage requires the reserved exposure measure.

\paragraph{The pooled test detects modest continuation.} The per-shock reversal correlations in
Table~\ref{tab:atlas} are small and mixed (mean $0.02$), which in isolation reads as roughly
efficient pricing. But the per-shock test is underpowered; pooling firms across shocks is not.
Table~\ref{tab:overreact} sorts all firm$\times$shock observations into announcement-CAR quintiles
and measures the subsequent $[+2,+10]$ drift. The pattern is monotone: the bottom quintile (biggest
losers on impact, mean CAR $-4.8\%$) drifts a further $-0.32\%$ ($t=-2.1$), the top quintile
(biggest winners, $+3.5\%$) drifts a further $+0.42\%$. Estimating the drift on quintile dummies
\emph{within} shock (shock fixed effects) with standard errors clustered by firm, the top-minus-bottom
spread is $+0.73\%$ ($t=4.0$) and the per-step slope is $+0.19\%$ per quintile ($t=5.0$); the
per-quintile $t$-statistics reported in the table are the simpler ``mean drift $\neq 0$'' tests.
Firms that moved most on impact kept moving the same way---modest
\emph{under-reaction}, not the over-reaction and reversal a contrarian model predicts. It is small
in magnitude (well under one percent over eight days) but statistically clear, and it is the pooled,
high-power counterpart to the SVB contagion signature: information from these shocks diffused over
several days rather than being fully impounded at the announcement. This nuances H3: pricing is
approximately efficient, but with a systematic drift in the direction of the news.

\paragraph{The SVB outlier and the mechanism behind pooled continuation.} The two propagation results
are complementary, not contradictory. The random-effects analysis says the \emph{average} shock has
no reliable drift while the shocks differ enormously ($I^2=99\%$); the pooled quintile test says that
\emph{within} shocks, the firms that moved most on impact tended to keep moving. The reconciliation is
that the continuation is concentrated in the shocks with real informational content that diffuses
slowly---most visibly SVB, where the impact and the drift reinforced each other ($+0.42$ reversal
correlation) as the regional-bank run propagated firm by firm over the following days (a single-shock pattern we
read descriptively, not as a formally tested cross-shock effect). This is the
contagion reading: a banking shock is not impounded in a single announcement return because the
identity of the next vulnerable institution is revealed gradually. Technology and trade shocks, whose
content is closer to a one-shot repricing, contribute far less to the pooled continuation. The pooled
test thus aggregates a genuine but shock-specific under-reaction rather than manufacturing one from
noise; and because it is estimated on $\sim$19{,}000 observations with firm-clustered errors, it has
the power the per-shock reversal correlations lack.

\paragraph{Size subsamples.} Table~\ref{tab:sizesplit} splits firms into within-shock size
terciles. Dispersion is largest for mid-cap firms ($4.3\%$) and smallest for large caps ($2.9\%$),
and the continuation drift is slightly stronger in the tails than the middle; the reversal
correlations are small in every tercile. The differentiation and continuation patterns are not
confined to the smallest or largest firms.

\section{Power: What the Atlas Can and Cannot Say}\label{sec:power}
A comparative atlas is only as credible as its account of its own limits. Two limits matter.
First, at the \emph{firm} level the design is very well powered: with $\sim$1{,}900 firms per shock,
the minimum detectable mean CAR at $80\%$ power is $0.16$--$0.37$ percentage points
(Table~\ref{tab:power}), so the near-zero mean announcement CARs are genuine, not underpowered. This
is why we build the paper on dispersion and pooled tests rather than on mean CARs. Second, at the
\emph{cross-shock} level the design is weak: $n=10$ gives $80\%$ power only for correlations above
$0.78$, which is why the $0.69$ scaling correlation is reported as suggestive and why the family
comparison (Table~\ref{tab:typebucket}) is descriptive. The atlas can make sharp firm-level and
pooled statements; it cannot, with ten shocks, make sharp statements about how shock-level
attributes trade off. Adding shocks---the atlas is built to accept them one column at a time---is the
direct remedy.

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}
\paragraph{Why the mean CAR is uninformative and the dispersion is not.} A recurring temptation in
event studies of macro shocks is to report the mean abnormal return and its $t$-statistic. Here that
statistic is close to zero \emph{by construction}: the market model subtracts the benchmark, and a
market-wide shock is largely the benchmark. What survives the benchmark is the cross-sectional
\emph{spread}---how differently firms loaded on the same news---and that is exactly the object the
differentiation axis measures. The placebo test in Table~\ref{tab:diff} disciplines this reading:
elevated spread could in principle be ordinary volatility, so we benchmark each event's spread
against the spread the same firms exhibit on hundreds of ordinary days. That a microstructure rule
(the tick) and a trade shock (the tariff) both clear the placebo bar, while procedural rules do not,
tells us the atlas is picking up genuine reallocation, not calendar noise.

\paragraph{Event-induced variance and cross-sectional correlation.} The two classic threats to
short-horizon event studies \citep{boehmer1991,kolari2010} are present here in acute form: a policy
shock mechanically raises return variance and induces strong cross-sectional correlation in abnormal
returns. We do not fight these with parametric corrections but design around them. The event-induced
variance is not a nuisance but our \emph{signal}---it is precisely the differentiation we measure,
and the placebo distribution provides its correct null. The cross-sectional correlation means the
per-shock cross-section is effectively far smaller than its $\sim$1{,}900 firms suggest, which is why
we never rest a conclusion on a single shock's cross-sectional $t$-statistic and instead lean on the
pooled firm$\times$shock tests with firm-clustered errors and on design-based resampling.

\paragraph{What the single event date does and does not cost us.} We use one calendar date per shock.
For the differentiation and propagation axes this is defensible: a one-day timing error attenuates
measured dispersion and drift toward zero, so our rejections (dispersion above placebo; monotone
drift) are conservative to date misspecification. Where timing matters most is \emph{anticipation}:
distinguishing pre-announcement leakage from noise requires knowing whether the event was pre- or
post-close and announcement versus effective. This is exactly the judgment the cohort reserves, and
it is why we decline to interpret the slightly negative average pre-window CAR
(Table~\ref{tab:metare}) as leakage.

\paragraph{Raw CARs.} We report CARs without winsorizing; a handful of firm-days carry extreme
two-day returns. Because our inferences rest on cross-sectional \emph{dispersion} benchmarked to a
placebo, on rank statistics, and on pooled regressions with firm-clustered errors---none of which a
few tails can drive---trimming would not change the conclusions, but readers should note the raw
scale.

\paragraph{Sample and generalizability.} The atlas covers the $\sim$2{,}000 largest U.S. firms, so it
speaks to how large-cap value was reallocated, not to micro-caps or private markets. The ten shocks
are a convenience sample of salient 2022--2025 events, not a random draw, so cross-shock statistics
describe this wave rather than policy shocks in general. Both limitations point the same way: the
atlas is a scaffold whose scientific value grows as the cohort adds shocks and firm-level exposure,
and whose present contribution is the disciplined, honest measurement of the wave we have.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}
Measured on strictly common footing, the 2022--2025 shocks differentiate firms to sharply different
degrees, and where they differentiate---the tariff, the Reg-NMS tick, the SVB collapse---they do so
through firm-specific, idiosyncratic repricing rather than a re-pricing of systematic risk. The
market's reaction is approximately efficient but carries a systematic sliver of under-reaction,
strongest around the banking-contagion shock. We are explicit that the ten-shock cross-section
cannot support the strong ``differentiation scales with size'' claim of the original draft: its
$0.69$ correlation is one influential shock away from $0.26$, its bootstrap interval includes zero,
and the design is underpowered for cross-shock correlations below $0.78$. What survives is more
useful because it is testable: differentiation is real and idiosyncratic, and generic observable
traits explain only about a fifth of it.

The remaining four-fifths is where the cohort's reserved contribution lives. Each of the ten shocks
is owned by the student whose individual paper studies it, and each of those papers produces a
\emph{named} firm-level exposure measure---import-input intensity for the tariffs, uninsured-deposit
and held-to-maturity exposure for SVB, AI-exposure for the language-model shocks, tick-constraint
eligibility for the Reg-NMS tick, and so on---together with the exact intraday event time. Assembling
these into a permno$\times$shock crosswalk turns the atlas's two central open questions into direct
tests: does named exposure explain the idiosyncratic dispersion that beta and size cannot, and was
that exposure priced \emph{before} the announcement? Those tests---not another robustness check on
the size proxy---are the natural next step, and the atlas has been built to receive them.
