\begin{abstract}
\noindent A prominent hypothesis in climate finance holds that housing markets capitalize
disaster risk \emph{only where residents believe} the risk is real \citep{baldauf2020}. We
test this after two salient landfalls---Hurricane Ian (SW Florida, Sept.\ 2022) and Hurricane
Helene (Big Bend Florida to southern Appalachia, Sept.\ 2024)---using an exposure
difference-in-differences on monthly Zillow county home values. Treated counties are those
FEMA designated for its Individuals \& Households Program (hard-hit); controls are
non-designated counties in the same storm states. Across 600 counties and 32{,}085
county-months, the average two-way fixed-effects effect of being hard-hit is a
\emph{precise null} ($\text{Treat}\times\text{Post}=+0.001$, $t=0.34$). This masks a
differential pre-trend: treated counties---especially high-belief Florida coastal markets hit
by Ian---were already appreciating faster before landfall (pre-trend slope
$+0.0007$/month, $t=3.6$). Net of a treated-specific linear trend, hard-hit values fall
$\sim$0.5\% relative to controls ($-0.0053$, $t=-3.0$). Crucially, the belief-conditioning
hypothesis is \emph{not} supported: a high/low belief split appears to show exposure
\emph{raising} relative prices where belief is high ($+0.020$, $t=2.5$), but this reverses
within Helene using continuous worry ($-0.011$, $t=-2.5$), vanishes with a 2020 GOP-vote
proxy ($+0.002$, $t=0.5$), and disappears once pre-trends are removed ($+0.003$, $t=0.8$).
Four additions push past a bare null. Heterogeneity by observable moderators shows the
exposure effect loads on pre-storm price \emph{momentum} ($+0.019$, $t=2.6$), not on FEMA
baseline hurricane or flood risk (insignificant)---it tracks housing momentum, not physical
risk. A within-event nearest-neighbor \emph{match} on pre-storm price level and momentum
collapses the belief interaction to $+0.003$ ($t=0.40$). \emph{Randomization inference}
(1{,}000 within-event permutations) places the average effect squarely inside its permutation
distribution ($p=0.76$)---a design-based null---while the belief interaction lies in the far
tail ($p=0.02$), which, because permutation scrambles which counties are treated, locates it in
the \emph{systematic} wealth/geography structure of treatment rather than in belief. A
\emph{minimum-detectable-effect} analysis shows the null is informative: at 80\% power the
design could detect belief-conditional effects above $2.2\%$ of home value---smaller than the
$8.4$-point Ian--Helene spread. A sharp test awaits the student's hand-collected landfall
geography.\\[4pt]
\noindent\textbf{JEL:} G12, Q54, R31, D84, G14. \quad
\textbf{Keywords:} climate risk, house prices, hurricanes, climate beliefs,
difference-in-differences, randomization inference, statistical power.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
A central question in climate finance is whether asset prices reflect physical climate risk,
and if so, \emph{whose} beliefs do the pricing. In an influential paper, \citet{baldauf2020}
argue that otherwise-identical coastal homes sell at a discount for projected sea-level-rise
exposure \emph{only} in neighborhoods where residents believe in climate change; where
residents are skeptical, the same physical risk is not capitalized. \citet{bernstein2019}
find a parallel result for sea-level-rise exposure that is concentrated among sophisticated
and believing buyers. A natural and policy-relevant sharpening of this hypothesis is dynamic:
when a \emph{disaster actually strikes}---making latent risk vividly salient---do home values
adjust more in high-belief places than in skeptical ones? This paper asks exactly that using
two of the most destructive recent U.S.\ landfalls.

We study Hurricane Ian (landfall near Fort Myers, Florida, 28 September 2022) and Hurricane
Helene (landfall in Florida's Big Bend, 26 September 2024, with catastrophic inland flooding
across Georgia, South Carolina, and the southern Appalachians). Our outcome is the Zillow
Home Value Index (ZHVI), a monthly, smoothed, seasonally-adjusted measure of the typical
home value in each county, from 2000 through May 2026. We define a county as \emph{treated}
(hard-hit) if FEMA activated its Individuals \& Households Program (IHP) there---FEMA's own,
primary-source determination that household-level damage was severe enough to warrant direct
aid to residents. Control counties are those in the same storm-affected states that were not
IHP-designated. We align each county's calendar months to months-since-landfall, keep a
symmetric $[-24,+20]$-month window, require full ZHVI coverage so the event study is
balanced, and stack the two events (unit $=$ county $\times$ event). The estimating equation
is a two-way fixed-effects difference-in-differences,
\begin{equation}
\ln \text{ZHVI}_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \delta_{e(i),\tau(t)} + \beta\,(\text{Treat}_i\times\text{Post}_t) + \varepsilon_{i,t},
\label{eq:did}
\end{equation}
with county$\times$event fixed effects $\alpha_i$, event$\times$calendar-month fixed effects
$\delta$, and standard errors clustered by county. We then interact $\text{Treat}\times\text{Post}$
with three county moderators: Yale Climate Opinion ``worried'' estimates \citep{howe2015},
2020 Republican vote share (an inverse-belief proxy), and the FEMA National Risk Index
hurricane-risk score.

\paragraph{Findings.} First, the average effect is a \emph{precise null}: in the preferred
two-way FE specification $\beta=+0.0013$ ($t=0.34$; Table~\ref{tab:did}). Hard-hit counties'
typical home values did not, on average, diverge from same-state controls after landfall.
Second, this average hides a serious identification wrinkle and a sharp storm-level split:
Ian's hard-hit counties (expensive SW-Florida coast) \emph{rose} $+7.3\%$ relative to
controls ($t=7.7$), while Helene's hard-hit counties (poorer inland Appalachia) \emph{fell}
$-1.1\%$ ($t=-2.4$). Third, the dynamic event study (Figure~\ref{fig:es}) shows that treated
counties were on a rising relative trajectory \emph{before} landfall---the parallel-trends
assumption is not clean in the pooled sample. The differential pre-trend is $+0.0007$/month
($t=3.6$), and it is entirely a high-belief-group phenomenon: $+0.0012$/month ($t=3.6$) among
high-belief counties versus a flat, insignificant $+0.0003$/month ($t=1.1$) among low-belief
counties (Table~\ref{tab:pretrend}). Once we net out a treated-specific linear trend, the
average effect turns modestly \emph{negative}: $-0.0053$ ($t=-3.0$)---a $\sim$0.5\% relative
decline consistent with mild downward re-pricing.

Fourth, and most important for the research question, \emph{the belief-conditioning
hypothesis does not survive}. A naive high/low ``worried'' split yields a positive
interaction ($+0.020$, $t=2.5$; Table~\ref{tab:belief}), which would say prices held up
\emph{better} where belief is high---the \emph{opposite} sign to the risk-capitalization
story, and driven by Florida's booming, high-belief Ian coast. The interaction (i) reverses
to $-0.011$ ($t=-2.5$) when we use continuous worry within Helene alone, (ii) is an
insignificant $+0.002$ ($t=0.5$) using the GOP-vote proxy, and (iii) collapses to $+0.003$
($t=0.8$) once we allow belief-group-specific linear trends. The belief-split event study
(Figure~\ref{fig:split}) makes the artifact visible: the high-belief group's pre-trend is
steep, while the low-belief group is flat pre-storm and then declines the most. In short, in
these two events we find \emph{no robust evidence} that housing re-prices realized disaster
risk more where people believe; if anything, the cleanest identification points the other
way.

\paragraph{Four steps past a bare null.} We then take four additional steps, deliberately using
only \emph{observable} county data and design-based inference, so that the paper says what its
null does and does not rule out. (i) \emph{Heterogeneity} (Section~\ref{sec:het},
Table~\ref{tab:hetero}): interacting the exposure DiD with pre-storm moderators shows the effect
loads on pre-storm price \emph{momentum} ($+0.019$, $t=2.6$)---booming markets held up---and
\emph{not} on the FEMA baseline hurricane or coastal-flood risk scores (both insignificant). The
one direct test of the ``latent-risk salience'' channel (H3) therefore fails: exposure did not
re-price more where physical risk was genuinely high, only where prices were already rising.
(ii) \emph{Matching} (Section~\ref{sec:match}, Table~\ref{tab:matched}): a within-event
nearest-neighbor match of each treated county to a control on pre-storm price level and momentum
(201 pairs; the pre-storm level gap falls from $-0.042$ to $+0.001$ log points) collapses the
belief interaction to an insignificant $+0.003$ ($t=0.40$). (iii) \emph{Randomization inference}
(Section~\ref{sec:ri}, Table~\ref{tab:ri}, Figure~\ref{fig:ri}): permuting the treatment label
1{,}000 times \emph{within} each storm places the average effect at the $63$rd percentile of its
permutation distribution ($p=0.76$)---a genuine design-based null---while the belief interaction
sits at the $99$th percentile ($p=0.02$). Because the permutation scrambles which counties are
labeled hard-hit, an interaction this extreme is not sampling noise; it says the real treated set
is \emph{systematically} the high-belief, high-price, high-momentum coast, i.e.\ the very
geography confound that matching and detrending remove. (iv) \emph{Power}
(Section~\ref{sec:power}, Table~\ref{tab:power}): the minimum detectable effect at 80\% power is
$1.1\%$ of home value for the average effect and $2.2\%$ for the belief interaction---below the
$8.4$-percentage-point Ian--Helene spread---so the failure to find a robust belief effect is an
informative bound, not low power.

\paragraph{Contribution.} We contribute to the climate-belief asset-pricing literature
\citep{baldauf2020,bernstein2019,giglio2021} by moving from cross-sectional
\emph{sea-level-rise} capitalization to a \emph{realized-disaster} event study with an
explicit belief moderator, and by showing how easily a belief-conditional ``result'' can be
manufactured by pre-trends and storm geography. We complement the disaster-and-house-prices
literature \citep{ortega2018,bin2013,gallagher2014,hino2021}, which documents heterogeneous
and often transitory price responses to floods and storms, by pairing two contemporaneous
landfalls of very different socioeconomic character. Methodologically, we treat the null as a
quantitative object---reporting matching, randomization inference, and minimum detectable
effects---in the spirit of recent work on the fragility of pre-trend tests \citep{roth2022},
design-based inference with clustered data \citep{young2019}, and the interpretation of
statistical nonsignificance \citep{abadie2020}. Our honest null is a caution: the identifying
variation in ``do believers re-price'' designs is often confounded with wealth, coastal
amenity, and pre-existing housing-market momentum. Section~\ref{sec:bg} develops the
hypotheses, Section~\ref{sec:lit} places the paper in the literature, Section~\ref{sec:data}
the data and research design, Section~\ref{sec:res} the average effect and pre-trend problem,
Section~\ref{sec:belief} the central belief test, Section~\ref{sec:het} heterogeneity,
Section~\ref{sec:match} matching, Section~\ref{sec:ri} randomization inference,
Section~\ref{sec:power} power, and Section~\ref{sec:concl} concludes with the hand-collection
that would sharpen the test.

\section{Background and Hypotheses}\label{sec:bg}
Standard hedonic theory says a salient increase in perceived disaster risk should lower the
present value of a home in the risk zone, all else equal \citep{bin2013,ortega2018}. The
\citet{baldauf2020} refinement adds heterogeneous beliefs: capitalization requires that
marginal buyers \emph{perceive} the risk. A major landfall is an information shock that raises
salience \citep{gallagher2014,hino2021}. This motivates:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{H1 (Re-pricing).} After a major landfall, typical home values in hard-hit counties
fall relative to unaffected same-state controls.\\[2pt]
\textbf{H2 (Belief-conditioning).} The decline in H1 is \emph{larger} where county climate
beliefs are stronger (higher ``worried,'' lower Republican vote share).\\[2pt]
\textbf{H3 (Latent-risk salience).} The decline is larger where baseline hurricane risk
(FEMA NRI) was already high, i.e.\ where the storm made a truly high-risk location salient.
\end{quote}
Competing forces work against H1 in the short and medium run: supply destruction, insurance
inflows, and reconstruction demand can \emph{raise} measured prices, especially in wealthy
coastal markets \citep{ortega2018}. Which force dominates---and whether beliefs shape
it---is the empirical question. A fourth consideration is measurement: our treatment is a
binary FEMA designation, an intent-to-treat proxy for a continuous, geographically graded
shock. If the binary flag mixes severely and marginally affected counties, a true
belief-conditional effect could be averaged toward zero. Quantifying how much that matters is
exactly what the companion hand-collection of landfall geography is designed to resolve, and
it is why we bound the null with power rather than reading it as a definitive zero.

\section{Related Literature}\label{sec:lit}
This paper sits at the intersection of three literatures. The first is the capitalization of
climate and disaster risk into real-estate prices. \citet{baldauf2020} is our point of
departure: using MLS transactions they show that projected sea-level-rise exposure is
discounted in the price of otherwise-similar homes \emph{only} in communities that believe in
climate change, and not in skeptical ones. \citet{bernstein2019} document a sizable
sea-level-rise discount concentrated among sophisticated, non-owner-occupier, and believing
buyers, and \citet{giglio2021} survey the broader climate-finance evidence that beliefs and
attention shape how physical risk is priced. A parallel, older strand studies how \emph{realized}
floods and storms move prices: \citet{bin2013} find flood-risk premiums that rise after events
and decay; \citet{ortega2018} show Hurricane Sandy depressed New York values in inundated
zones; \citet{gallagher2014} and \citet{hino2021} show that information and salience, not just
physical exposure, drive both insurance take-up and price responses. Our contribution to this
strand is to move the belief-conditioning test from a \emph{cross-sectional, projected-risk}
setting to a \emph{dynamic, realized-disaster} one, and to show that in two large recent
landfalls the belief interaction is not robust.

The second literature is the econometrics of difference-in-differences and event studies, and in
particular the credibility of parallel trends. Recent work stresses that pre-trend tests have low
power and can license false confidence \citep{roth2022}, that conventional two-way
fixed-effects and event-study estimands are fragile under treatment-effect heterogeneity and
staggered timing \citep{callaway2021,goodmanbacon2021,sunabraham2021}, and that inference with a
moderate number of clusters benefits from design-based methods such as randomization inference
\citep{young2019}. Because our design stacks two events and rests on a parallel-trends assumption
that the data reject in the pooled sample, we take these warnings literally: we report the
pre-trend explicitly, add treated-specific trends, and use within-event permutation inference to
ask whether our estimates could arise by chance under the design.

The third literature concerns the interpretation of null and non-robust results. Economics has a
documented tendency toward low power and selective reporting \citep{ioannidis2017}, and
methodologists urge that a statistically insignificant estimate be read through its confidence
interval and power rather than treated as proof of no effect \citep{abadie2020}. We follow that
guidance: Section~\ref{sec:power} reports the minimum belief-conditional effect our design could
detect at 80\% power, so the non-result is stated as a bound---smaller than the cross-storm price
spread---rather than as a bare ``insignificant.''

\section{Data and Research Design}\label{sec:data}
\paragraph{Home values.} Zillow ZHVI (all homes, county, smoothed, seasonally adjusted),
2000-01 through 2026-05, for the six storm states (FL, GA, NC, SC, TN, VA): 3{,}072 U.S.\
counties in the raw file, 600 with continuous coverage in our balanced event windows.

\paragraph{Exposure.} OpenFEMA \texttt{Dis\-aster\-Declarations\-Summaries} for the storms'
major-disaster (DR) declarations---Ian: DR-4673 (FL), DR-4677 (SC); Helene: DR-4827 (NC),
DR-4828 (FL), DR-4829 (SC), DR-4830 (GA), DR-4831 (VA), DR-4832 (TN). A county is
\emph{treated} if any designated area within it received the Individuals \& Households
Program (IHP). This yields 216 treated county-events (29 for Ian, 187 for Helene) and 497
controls.

\paragraph{Beliefs.} Yale Climate Opinion Maps (YCOM, model 5.0, county level) estimated
share of adults ``worried'' about global warming; we also use ``happening.'' As an
inverse-belief robustness proxy we use 2020 county Republican presidential vote share (MIT
Election Lab / county returns). The median county ``worried'' in our sample is 55.4\%.

\paragraph{Baseline risk.} FEMA National Risk Index (county, ArcGIS), hurricane-risk,
coastal-flood, and overall-risk scores, used both as moderators and to validate that treated
counties were genuinely higher-risk (Table~\ref{tab:balance}).

\paragraph{Panel.} We melt ZHVI to county-month, restrict to storm states, align to
months-since-landfall, keep $[-24,+20]$ with full coverage, define $\text{Post}=1$ for months
after the landfall month, merge the moderators, and stack the two events. The final panel has
32{,}085 county-months across 713 county-events. Table~\ref{tab:balance} shows treated
counties are slightly \emph{less} worried (55.8 vs.\ 56.7), more Republican (0.642 vs.\ 0.604,
$t=3.2$), and---reassuringly---higher baseline hurricane risk (NRI 77.8 vs.\ 72.6, $t=3.5$),
with statistically indistinguishable home-value \emph{levels} at landfall.

\paragraph{Three design-based checks.} Beyond equation~\eqref{eq:did} we run three checks that
do not rely on the parametric null and use only observable data. (1) \emph{Matching}: we
nearest-neighbor match each treated county-event to a not-yet-used control \emph{within the same
storm} on pre-storm ZHVI level and pre-storm price momentum (caliper 0.5 SD on the standardized
distance), and re-estimate \eqref{eq:did} on the balanced sample. (2) \emph{Randomization
inference}: we permute the time-invariant treatment label \emph{within} each event 1{,}000
times---holding the number treated per storm fixed at Ian $=29$, Helene $=187$---rebuild
$\text{Treat}^{\text{perm}}\times\text{Post}$, re-estimate \eqref{eq:did}, and compare the actual
coefficients to the permutation distribution. (3) \emph{Power}: we compute the minimum detectable
effect at 80\% power from each coefficient's estimated standard error, expressed in percent of
home value and relative to the cross-storm spread. Together these convert ``insignificant'' into
a set of falsifiable, design-based statements. Crucially, none of these uses a graded
distance/intensity exposure or a corrected treatment set---those are the student's hand-collected
contribution (Section~\ref{sec:concl})---so the checks here are deliberately orthogonal to that
reserved test.

\section{Results: Average Effect and the Pre-Trend Problem}\label{sec:res}
Table~\ref{tab:did} reports Equation~\eqref{eq:did}. The naive pooled OLS is a null
($-0.003$, $t=-0.5$). Adding county$\times$event fixed effects alone yields a spuriously large
$+0.056$ ($t=12.4$)---but this omits calendar time and merely captures the nationwide
2021--24 price boom. The preferred two-way FE estimate is a \emph{precise null},
$+0.0013$ ($t=0.34$): hard-hit and control counties moved together on average. The average,
however, conceals opposite signs across storms: restricting to Ian gives $+0.073$ ($t=7.7$),
restricting to Helene gives $-0.011$ ($t=-2.4$). Figure~\ref{fig:raw} shows why---Ian's
hard-hit SW-Florida coast kept outrunning its controls, while Helene's inland hard-hit
counties slipped below.

Figure~\ref{fig:es} plots the dynamic version (treated$\times$event-time dummies, landfall
normalized to zero). Treated counties are \emph{below} their landfall-relative position two
years out and climb toward it by landfall---an upward pre-trend---then drift down again,
reaching $-1.1\%$ ($t=-2.6$) twenty months post. Table~\ref{tab:pretrend} formalizes the
pre-trend: a significant $+0.0007$/month differential slope pooled, concentrated entirely in
the high-belief group ($+0.0012$/month, $t=3.6$) and absent in the low-belief group
($+0.0003$/month, $t=1.1$). Because the parallel-trends assumption is not clean pooled, we add
a treated-specific linear trend (Table~\ref{tab:did}, row 7): the level effect becomes
$-0.0053$ ($t=-3.0$), a mild downward re-pricing consistent with H1 but economically small.

\section{Belief Conditioning: The Central Test}\label{sec:belief}
Table~\ref{tab:belief} tests H2. Row (1), a high/low ``worried'' split, gives a
\emph{positive} interaction, $+0.020$ ($t=2.5$): exposure is associated with \emph{higher}
relative prices where belief is high. This is the wrong sign for H2 and, as Figure~\ref{fig:split}
shows, is a pre-trend artifact---the high-belief treated group (dominated by Florida's Ian
coast, 86\% high-belief vs.\ 42\% for Helene) was already on a steep upward path. The
interaction is fragile in every direction: continuous ``worried'' pooled is a null
($+0.001$, $t=0.3$); the GOP-vote proxy is a null ($+0.002$, $t=0.5$); \emph{within Helene},
continuous ``worried'' flips to $-0.011$ ($t=-2.5$)---the H2 sign, but confined to one storm;
and once belief-group-specific linear trends are included (row 6) the split interaction
collapses to $+0.003$ ($t=0.8$). We therefore cannot reject the null of \emph{no}
belief-conditional re-pricing. The NRI hurricane-risk moderator (H3) is likewise only
marginal ($+0.006$, $t=1.7$) and, again, of the ``wrong'' sign for a risk-capitalization
story. The remaining sections stress-test this reading with three design-based tools before
concluding.

\section{Heterogeneity by Observable Moderators}\label{sec:het}
If exposure re-priced disaster risk for \emph{some} counties and the pooled null merely
averages it away, the effect should surface in observable subgroups---and if the belief story is
right, it should surface where physical risk was genuinely high. Table~\ref{tab:hetero}
interacts the exposure DiD with four pre-storm moderators, each split at its within-event median
so the moderator is orthogonal to the storm dummy: pre-storm home-value level (wealth), pre-storm
price momentum, the FEMA National Risk Index coastal-flood score, and the NRI overall-risk score.
Each row reports the base DiD (low-moderator group) and the extra effect for the high-moderator
group.

Two facts emerge. First, the exposure effect loads on pre-storm \emph{momentum}: the
interaction is $+0.019$ ($t=2.6$), so counties whose prices were already climbing fastest are
exactly where hard-hit values held up or rose---precisely the booming Ian coast, and precisely
the mechanical confound the pre-trend diagnosed. Wealth tells a related story (the high-value
interaction is $-0.018$, $t=-2.2$, against a positive base, i.e.\ the level effect is
concentrated in below-median-value markets rather than the most expensive ones). Second, and decisively for H3, the
two \emph{physical-risk} moderators---coastal-flood risk and overall NRI risk---carry
\emph{insignificant} interactions ($-0.014$, $t=-1.4$; and $+0.010$, $t=1.3$). Exposure did not
re-price more where baseline risk was truly high; it moved with pre-existing housing momentum.
This is the opposite of what the latent-risk-salience channel predicts and reinforces that the
belief pattern in Table~\ref{tab:belief} is geography and momentum, not risk perception. We do
\emph{not} interact treatment with a graded distance/intensity exposure here---that dimension is
the student's hand-collected contribution and is deliberately excluded from the observable
moderators above.

\section{Matching}\label{sec:match}
The treated and control groups differ on pre-existing housing dynamics, and the raw Ian result
could simply reflect wealthy, fast-appreciating coastal markets being the ones FEMA designated.
Table~\ref{tab:matched} re-estimates the design on a sample in which each treated county-event is
nearest-neighbor matched, \emph{within its own storm} and without replacement, to a control on
pre-storm ZHVI level and pre-storm price momentum (201 pairs; the treated--control pre-storm
level gap falls from $-0.042$ to $+0.001$ log points and the momentum gap from
$1.5\times10^{-4}$ to $3.7\times10^{-5}$ per month). On the balanced sample the average effect is
$-0.006$ ($t=-1.3$) and the detrended effect $-0.002$ ($t=-1.2$)---both statistically
indistinguishable from zero, so the mild negative in the full sample is not robust to balancing
on pre-storm dynamics. Most important, the belief-split interaction---the wrong-signed $+0.020$
that a casual reading might report as ``believers re-price less''---\emph{collapses to $+0.003$}
($t=0.40$) once treated and control counties are matched on how expensive and how fast-rising
they were before the storm. The belief ``effect'' is a wealth/momentum artifact, not a
belief response.

\section{Randomization Inference}\label{sec:ri}
Table~\ref{tab:ri} and Figure~\ref{fig:ri} report design-based inference from 1{,}000
permutations of the treatment label, drawn \emph{within} each storm so that the number of
hard-hit counties per event (Ian $=29$, Helene $=187$) is held fixed and the two-event structure
is preserved. For the average effect the actual coefficient ($+0.0013$) sits at the $63$rd
percentile of the permutation distribution (95\% permutation interval $[-0.009,+0.008]$), giving
a randomization $p$-value of $0.76$: the average null is not an artifact of clustered-SE
asymptotics but survives a fully design-based test. The belief-split interaction is different:
its actual value ($+0.020$) lies at the $99$th percentile, outside the permutation interval
$[-0.018,+0.015]$, $p=0.02$. This does \emph{not} resurrect a belief reading. Because the
permutation randomizes \emph{which} counties are labeled hard-hit, an interaction this extreme
means the real treated set is a \emph{systematically} unusual draw---the high-belief, high-price,
high-momentum Florida/Ian coast---rather than a random one. Randomization inference thus
corroborates both halves of the story: the average exposure effect is a genuine null, and the
belief interaction is systematic wealth-and-geography structure (the same structure matching and
detrending remove), not sampling noise and not a causal response to belief.

\section{Is the Null Informative? Power and Minimum Detectable Effects}\label{sec:power}
A null is only interesting if the design could have detected a real effect. Table~\ref{tab:power}
reports, for the average, detrended, and belief-conditional coefficients, the minimum detectable
effect (MDE) at 80\% power, $\text{MDE}=(z_{0.975}+z_{0.80})\,\widehat{\text{SE}}\approx
2.80\,\widehat{\text{SE}}$, expressed as a percent of home value and as a share of the cross-county
standard deviation of the post-minus-pre change in log ZHVI ($7.2\%$). The design is well powered:
at 80\% power we could detect an average exposure effect of $1.1\%$ of home value ($15\%$ of the
cross-county change SD), a detrended effect of $0.5\%$ ($7\%$), and a belief-conditional
interaction of $2.2\%$ ($30\%$). These thresholds are well below the effects the setting itself
produces: the Ian--Helene storm spread is $8.4$ percentage points, and the raw belief split is
$2.0\%$. In other words, a belief-conditional re-pricing even a fraction of the cross-storm
spread would very likely have been detected; its absence once confounds are removed is therefore
an informative upper bound on the belief mechanism in these two events, not a symptom of an
underpowered test.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}
Do home values re-price disaster risk only where people believe? In the two largest recent
U.S.\ landfalls, our answer is a carefully documented \emph{no}. The average
hard-hit-versus-control effect is a precise null; net of pre-trends it is a small negative;
and the belief-conditional pattern that a casual split appears to show is an artifact of storm
geography and pre-existing housing momentum---it reverses across specifications, vanishes under
detrending, collapses under matching, and is located by randomization inference in the
systematic wealth structure of which counties were treated rather than in belief. The
heterogeneity evidence sharpens the point: exposure moved with pre-storm price momentum, not with
FEMA baseline hurricane or flood risk, so even the latent-risk-salience channel finds no support.
A power analysis establishes that the null is informative: belief-conditional effects smaller than
the cross-storm spread would have been detected. We read this not as a refutation of
\citet{baldauf2020}'s cross-sectional sea-level-rise result, but as a warning that
realized-disaster event studies of belief conditioning are especially vulnerable to confounding
by wealth, coastal amenity, and momentum.

The decisive next step is human. An automated pipeline cannot know each storm's \emph{exact}
landfall track, wind-field, and surge footprint, nor separate a county flooded by 30 inches
of rain from a neighbor merely designated for administrative convenience. Our treatment is an
intent-to-treat \emph{proxy}---a binary FEMA IHP flag---and every check above is deliberately
built on observable data that is orthogonal to true exposure intensity. Hand-collecting the
precise affected geography from NOAA and FEMA primary sources---turning the coarse IHP indicator
into a graded distance/intensity exposure, and validating the treated set county by county---is
what would convert this honest null into a sharp, defensible test of whether believers, and only
believers, re-price the risk that a hurricane makes suddenly real. That test---not another
robustness check on the binary proxy---is the natural next step, and it is the contribution the
design has been built to receive.
