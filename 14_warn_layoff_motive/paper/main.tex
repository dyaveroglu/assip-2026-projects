\begin{abstract}
\noindent Firms lay off workers for very different reasons---to cut costs in a
downturn, or to reallocate away from a shrinking line of business---and it is not
obvious that equity investors treat the two alike. We build a multi-state panel
of mass-layoff (WARN Act) notices for 2022--2025 from the California, Texas, and
Oregon WARN databases (6{,}560 notices), fuzzy-match the filers to listed firms,
and estimate market-model cumulative abnormal returns (CARs) around 615
firm-level layoff events at 328 public companies. On average the market gives a
\emph{modest but statistically significant negative} verdict: CAR$[0,+5]=-1.6\%$
($t=-2.41$), concentrated in the days after the notice, with a clean near-zero
pre-event placebo ($-0.18\%$, $t=-0.32$). But the average hides sharp
heterogeneity, and it is not about \emph{how many} people are let go. Absolute
headcount is irrelevant ($t=0.46$); what matters is the layoff's size
\emph{relative to the firm} and, equivalently, how small the firm is. Small firms
lose $3.9\%$ ($t=-2.86$) while large firms are essentially unaffected ($+0.4\%$,
$t=1.13$); a one-standard-deviation larger relative layoff moves CAR by
$-3.3$ percentage points ($t=-2.77$), and this punishment falls almost entirely
on small firms (size$\times$relative-layoff interaction $=-0.065$, $t=-2.86$).
Four journal-track additions sharpen the reading. Randomization inference
(2{,}000 permutations) places the materiality slope far in the tail (design-based
$p=0.001$), so the effect does not rest on clustered-standard-error asymptotics.
The average reaction survives three alternative abnormal-return benchmarks and is,
if anything, \emph{stronger} against market- and S\&P-adjusted returns
($-2.0\%$, $t=-3.0$). Crucially, our crude machine-readable proxy for
\emph{motive}---whether the filing is a plant \emph{closure} (exit/reallocation)
or an ongoing-operation \emph{layoff} (cost-cutting)---shows \emph{no} difference
in CARs ($t=-0.07$; randomization $p=0.92$), and a minimum-detectable-effect
analysis shows this null is \emph{not} a tight bound: the machine proxy could only
have detected a motive effect of $5.1$ percentage points at 80\% power, far above
plausible layoff-CAR magnitudes. The machine motive flag is thus both a point-null
\emph{and} too imprecise to rule out a real motive effect. Distinguishing
cost-cutting from strategic reallocation therefore requires reading the
announcements and hand-coding motive, which is the central task this paper sets
up. \\[4pt]
\noindent\textbf{JEL:} G14, J63, M51, G34. \quad
\textbf{Keywords:} mass layoffs, WARN Act, event study, abnormal returns,
downsizing, restructuring, randomization inference, statistical power.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
When a firm announces that it is cutting jobs, the stock can go up or down, and
which way it goes has long been read as a window into \emph{why} the firm is
cutting. A layoff undertaken to trim fat and defend margins may be cheered as
discipline; a layoff forced by collapsing demand is a bad-news signal about the
franchise; and a layoff that reallocates resources out of a dying business toward
a growing one is something else again \citep{cascio1993,love2005}. The empirical
record on the average reaction is famously mixed---early studies found small
negative reactions \citep{worrell1991,palmon1997}, later work found the reaction
attenuating or turning positive over time \citep{farber2009}, and the sign
appears to depend on the layoff's stated \emph{cause}
\citep{elayan1998,nixon2004,chen2001}. This paper asks a sharp version of the
question using a data source that has been under-used in finance: the mandatory
mass-layoff notices filed under the Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification
(WARN) Act \citep{addison1994}.

Our question is: \emph{across mass-layoff events at public firms, what kind of
layoff does the market punish---and can the announced form of the layoff tell us
the firm's motive?} We answer it with a cross-sectional event study. We assemble
every WARN notice filed in California, Texas, and Oregon from 2022 through 2025
(6{,}560 notices covering 3{,}450 distinct filers), fuzzy-match the filer names to
CRSP/Compustat public firms, cluster each firm's site-level notices into
firm-level layoff \emph{events}, and estimate market-model CARs against the CRSP
value-weighted index. The matched sample with valid returns is 615 firm-events at
328 listed companies over 2022--2024.\footnote{The CRSP daily market index in our
WRDS extract runs through 2024-12-31, so events with a full post-event window are
those through December 2024; the WARN panel itself extends into 2025.}

Our findings are threefold, and each is then stress-tested with a design-based
method. \emph{First}, the average market reaction to a WARN notice is negative and
significant, but small. Over the $[0,+5]$ window the mean CAR is $-1.6\%$
($t=-2.41$; Table~\ref{tab:cars}); the $[0,+2]$ window is $-1.3\%$ ($t=-2.22$);
the reaction sits after the notice date, and the pre-event $[-10,-2]$ placebo is
an insignificant $-0.18\%$. Notably only $49\%$ of CARs are negative: the mean is
driven by a negative \emph{tail}, not a uniform markdown. The market does not
reflexively punish layoffs.

\emph{Second}, what determines who lands in that negative tail is \emph{firm
size / relative layoff size}, not the raw number of jobs. The absolute headcount
laid off carries no information for returns (Table~\ref{tab:main} column 2,
$t=0.46$). The layoff's size \emph{relative to the firm's workforce} does: a
one-standard-deviation increase in the relative-layoff ratio is associated with a
$-3.3$-percentage-point CAR ($t=-2.77$, column 1). Equivalently, larger firms are
spared: a one-SD increase in $\ln(\text{assets})$ moves CAR by $+3.1$pp
($t=3.34$, column 3). The two are two sides of one coin---a WARN filing is a
locally large but firm-level tiny event for a mega-cap, and a material event for a
small company. Splitting the sample makes this vivid (Table~\ref{tab:groups}):
small firms lose $3.9\%$ ($t=-2.86$) and large firms gain a statistically-zero
$0.4\%$; top-half relative layoffs lose $3.5\%$ ($t=-2.70$) and bottom-half
relative layoffs are flat. The punishment is concentrated where the layoff is big
relative to the firm: the size$\times$relative-layoff interaction is $-0.065$
($t=-2.86$; Table~\ref{tab:hetero}).

\emph{Third}, and most important for the paper's title question, our
machine-readable proxy for \emph{motive} does \emph{not} separate winners from
losers. State WARN filings flag whether a layoff is a permanent plant
\emph{closure}---a natural proxy for exit/reallocation---or an ongoing-operation
\emph{layoff}---a natural proxy for cost-cutting. Closures and ongoing-operation
layoffs earn statistically identical CARs ($-1.5\%$ vs.\ $-1.6\%$; the closure
dummy is $-0.001$ with $t=-0.07$ in the full specification,
Table~\ref{tab:main} column 5). The form of the layoff, as coded from the filing's
category field, is uninformative about motive in a way that moves prices.

We then push each result past the point where most short event studies stop, using
four design-based additions that are the methodological contribution of this
version of the paper. (i) \emph{Randomization inference} (Section~\ref{sec:ri},
Table~\ref{tab:randinf}, Figure~\ref{fig:ri}): permuting the materiality regressor
2{,}000 times places the actual slope far outside the permutation distribution
(design-based $p=0.001$), while the closure/motive coefficient sits dead-center
($p=0.92$)---the materiality effect is real and the machine-motive null is robust
to design-based inference, not an artifact of clustered-SE asymptotics with a
moderate number of firms. (ii) \emph{Alternative abnormal-return benchmarks}
(Section~\ref{sec:altbench}, Table~\ref{tab:altbench}): re-deriving CARs from the
raw daily returns under market-adjusted, S\&P-500-adjusted, and mean-adjusted
models leaves the average negative reaction and the materiality slope intact---and
the market- and S\&P-adjusted reactions are actually \emph{larger} ($-2.0\%$,
$t\approx-3.0$). (iii) \emph{Expanded heterogeneity on observable moderators}
(Section~\ref{sec:het}, Table~\ref{tab:heteroext}): the materiality effect does
not concentrate in any observable subgroup (valuation, asset turnover, sample year,
goods-producing), reinforcing that it is a broad materiality phenomenon rather than
a proxy for something firm-specific. (iv) \emph{Power and minimum detectable
effects} (Section~\ref{sec:power}, Table~\ref{tab:power}): the materiality estimate
($-2.5$pp size-controlled, $-3.3$pp unconditional) is comparable to its
$\approx3.9$pp 80\%-power minimum detectable effect---the design is powered to
detect roughly the effect it finds---whereas the machine motive proxy could only
have detected a motive effect of $5.1$ percentage points at 80\% power---larger
than the entire average reaction and than any plausible motive effect. So the
closure flag is not merely \emph{zero}; it is \emph{too coarse and too noisy} to
rule out a real motive effect. This is exactly the wedge that motivates
hand-coding: the difference between ``we are closing this plant because the product
is dying'' (reallocation) and ``we are closing this plant to cut costs''
(retrenchment) is not in the WARN category field---it is in the announcement text
and the surrounding disclosure, which a human must read. Resolving true motive, and
re-running the cross-section on it, is the pivotal contribution we leave to the
student researcher (\texttt{STUDENT\_TASKS.md}).

We make four contributions. First, relative to the classic layoff event-study
literature \citep{worrell1991,palmon1997,chen2001,farber2009}, we bring a new and
cleaner event source---mandatory WARN filings with precise dates---and show that in
a modern (2022--2024) sample the average reaction is small and negative but the
action is entirely in the \emph{relative} size of the cut. Second, relative to the
strategy literature on downsizing motive
\citep{cascio1993,nixon2004,love2005,guthrie2008}, we show---and quantify with a
power calculation---that the coarse closure-vs-layoff distinction available in
administrative data is \emph{not} a usable proxy for the
cost-cutting-vs-reallocation motive that theory says should matter. Third, relative
to work using WARN data \citep{addison1994}, we provide, to our knowledge, one of
the first matched WARN-to-CRSP event studies. Fourth, we treat both the headline
effect and the null as quantitative objects, reporting design-based
(randomization) $p$-values, alternative-benchmark robustness, and minimum
detectable effects, so that readers can see what the design can and cannot rule
out \citep{young2019,abadie2020}. Section~\ref{sec:lit} places the paper in the
literature; Section~\ref{sec:hyp} develops hypotheses; Section~\ref{sec:data}
describes the data and matching; Section~\ref{sec:design} the research design and
inference; Section~\ref{sec:res} the main results; Section~\ref{sec:het}
heterogeneity; Section~\ref{sec:ri} randomization inference;
Section~\ref{sec:altbench} alternative benchmarks; Section~\ref{sec:power} power
and the informativeness of the motive null; Section~\ref{sec:robust} robustness;
and Section~\ref{sec:concl} concludes.

\section{Related Literature}\label{sec:lit}
This paper connects three strands. The first is the \emph{layoff event-study}
literature. Early studies of the stock-price reaction to layoff and plant-closing
announcements generally found small negative average returns
\citep{worrell1991,palmon1997}, with the reaction depending on the stated reason
for the cut: layoffs framed as demand-driven or financially-distressed drew more
negative reactions than layoffs framed as efficiency-improving or strategic
reorganizations \citep{elayan1998,chen2001,nixon2004}. \citet{farber2009} document
that the average reaction attenuated and at times turned positive over the
1970--1999 period, consistent with layoffs being increasingly read as proactive
restructuring rather than distress. Our contribution to this strand is a new event
source---mandatory WARN filings, which carry precise, legally-required dates and a
site-level headcount---and a modern (2022--2024) sample in which we show that the
average masks a materiality gradient: the reaction is entirely about the size of
the cut \emph{relative to the firm}, not the number of workers or the raw event.

The second strand is the \emph{strategy and management} literature on the
consequences and interpretation of downsizing. \citet{cascio1993} and
\citet{love2005} distinguish reactive, cost-cutting ``retrenchment'' from proactive,
strategic ``reorientation,'' and argue that the two have different performance
consequences; \citet{guthrie2008} show the effect of downsizing on firm performance
is moderated by industry conditions. This literature is precisely about
\emph{motive}. Our contribution is to test whether motive is legible in the one
motive-like field available in administrative data---the plant-closure-vs-layoff
category---and to show, with a power calculation, that it is not: the machine flag
is both a point-null and too imprecise to rule out the motive effect this
literature predicts. Measuring motive therefore requires the narrative, which
motivates the hand-coding step.

The third strand is \emph{event-study methodology and the interpretation of null
results}. We follow the standard market-model event-study framework
\citep{mackinlay1997,brown1985} and, because event-induced variance and
cross-sectional correlation can distort conventional $t$-tests
\citep{boehmer1991,corrado1989,kolari2010}, we complement parametric inference with
randomization inference \citep{young2019}, which is valid under the sharp null
without asymptotic assumptions on the number of clusters. We also follow the
guidance that a statistically insignificant estimate should be interpreted through
its power and minimum detectable effect rather than treated as proof of no effect
\citep{abadie2020}: Section~\ref{sec:power} states the motive null as a bound
($5.1$pp at 80\% power) rather than a bare ``insignificant,'' which is what makes
the case for hand-coding quantitative rather than rhetorical.

\section{Hypotheses}\label{sec:hyp}
Layoff announcements carry two offsetting signals \citep{palmon1997,chen2001}. A
cut can be \emph{good news}---evidence that management is disciplined and is
lowering the cost base (an ``efficiency'' signal)---or \emph{bad news}---evidence
that demand for the firm's product is falling (a ``demand'' signal). Which
dominates should depend on how material the cut is and on why it is being made.
\begin{quote}
\textbf{H1 (Average reaction).} The average CAR around a WARN mass-layoff notice
is non-zero. (Sign is an empirical question: the efficiency and demand signals
push in opposite directions.)
\end{quote}
A cut that is large relative to the firm is more likely to be read as a demand
signal about the whole franchise, whereas a cut that is trivial relative to the
firm is noise.
\begin{quote}
\textbf{H2 (Materiality).} CARs are decreasing in the size of the layoff
\emph{relative to the firm} (relative-layoff ratio), and, equivalently,
increasing in firm size. Absolute headcount, which does not scale by the firm,
should matter less.
\end{quote}
Finally, the motive. A layoff that \emph{reallocates} resources away from a
declining business (often a plant closure) toward better uses may be read
differently from a defensive, across-the-board \emph{cost-cutting} layoff
\citep{love2005,nixon2004}.
\begin{quote}
\textbf{H3 (Motive).} CARs differ by layoff motive. If the administrative
closure-vs-layoff flag captures motive, closures and ongoing-operation layoffs
should earn different CARs; if it does not, the flag will be uninformative and
motive must be measured from the announcement itself.
\end{quote}

\section{Data and Variables}\label{sec:data}
\paragraph{WARN notices.} The WARN Act requires employers with 100+ workers to
give 60 days' notice of a plant closing or mass layoff; states publish the
notices. We use three machine-readable state sources: the California EDD detailed
WARN reports (four fiscal-year files, 07/2022--06/2026, parsed from the official
PDF/XLSX), the Texas Workforce Commission WARN dataset
(\texttt{data.texas.gov}), and the Oregon WARN dataset (\texttt{data.oregon.gov}).
Each notice carries the filer name, the notice date, the number of employees
affected, the location, and (in CA/OR) a layoff/closure type. Pooling and
restricting to 2022--2025 gives 6{,}560 notices from 3{,}450 distinct filer names
(CA 5{,}548; TX 815; OR 190; a few notices in other states appear in the Oregon
cross-file).

\paragraph{Matching to listed firms.} WARN filers are operating names at a job
site (``Amgen,'' ``Applied Materials, Inc.\ (557 E.\ Ca\ldots)''). We build a name
universe of U.S.\ common stocks active 2021--2026 from CRSP (\texttt{stocknames})
plus Compustat legal names (\texttt{company}) linked to permno via the
CRSP/Compustat link, normalize both sides (dropping legal suffixes, ``dba'' trade
names, and store/site numbers), and fuzzy-match each filer to the nearest listed
name with a token-set score, keeping a match only when it clears a strict score
\emph{and} a leading-anchor test (the filer name and the listed name share their
first distinctive token). This high-precision pass matches 738 distinct filer
names to 412 listed firms. It deliberately \emph{misses} brand/subsidiary filings
(``Pixar''$\to$Disney, ``Optum''$\to$UnitedHealth); resolving those and verifying
the automated matches is a hand task (\texttt{STUDENT\_TASKS.md}). Because the
event study rests entirely on these matches, this precision-over-recall choice is
deliberate: a false match injects an unrelated firm's return into the sample,
whereas a missed match merely omits an event. The hand-verification and
brand-rescue steps reserved for the student directly raise recall without
sacrificing precision.

\paragraph{From notices to events.} Firms often file several site notices within
days. We cluster a firm's notices into \emph{events}: a new event begins when a
notice is more than 20 calendar days after the current event's first notice; the
event date is the first notice in the cluster and headcount is the cluster sum.
This yields 852 firm-events at 412 firms. Merging Compustat fundamentals (most
recent fiscal year before the event: assets, employees, sales, market cap,
industry) and CRSP returns leaves 615 firm-events at 328 firms with a valid
market model.

\paragraph{Variables.} \emph{Relative layoff} $=$ cluster headcount $/$ total
firm employees. \emph{Firm size} $=\ln(\text{assets})$ (and $\ln(\text{market
cap})$ in robustness). \emph{Closure} is an indicator that the filing's type
field contains ``closure'' (our crude reallocation/exit proxy). \emph{Tech} is an
indicator for computer/electronics/software SIC codes. In the extended
heterogeneity analysis we add three further \emph{observable} moderators, all
measured at the pre-event fiscal year and all deliberately orthogonal to the
reserved hand-coded motive: market-to-assets ($\text{market cap}/\text{assets}$, a
glamour/growth proxy), asset turnover ($\text{sales}/\text{assets}$), and a
goods-producing (non-tech manufacturing) indicator. Continuous predictors are
winsorized at 1/99 and standardized, so a coefficient is the effect of a one-SD
increase, in CAR points. Table~\ref{tab:sumstats} reports summary statistics: the
median WARN event lays off 86 workers, $0.8\%$ of the firm's workforce, at a firm
with $\$8$B in assets---confirming these are locally large but firm-level small
cuts at sizable public companies.

\section{Research Design and Inference}\label{sec:design}
\paragraph{Event study.} Day 0 is the first trading day on/after the notice date.
We estimate the market model $R_{it}=\alpha_i+\beta_i R_{mt}+\varepsilon_{it}$ over
$[-252,-46]$ against the CRSP value-weighted index and cumulate abnormal returns
over several windows. The cross-sectional regressions take a CAR as the dependent
variable and firm/layoff characteristics as regressors, with standard errors
clustered by firm (some firms file multiple events).

\paragraph{Inference.} Cross-sectional event-study $t$-tests can be distorted by
event-induced variance and by cross-sectional correlation among abnormal returns
\citep{boehmer1991,corrado1989,kolari2010}. We take three precautions beyond
clustered standard errors. First, we report a pre-event $[-10,-2]$ placebo window,
which should be zero if the reaction is news rather than anticipation or a
pre-trend. Second, we subject the two coefficients that carry the paper---the
materiality slope and the machine-motive (closure) coefficient---to
\emph{randomization inference}: we permute the regressor across events 2{,}000
times and compare the actual coefficient to the permutation distribution, yielding
a design-based $p$-value that is valid under the sharp null without asymptotic
assumptions \citep{young2019}. Third, we re-derive the CARs under three
alternative abnormal-return benchmarks straight from the raw daily returns, so the
result cannot be an artifact of the market-model specification. Finally, we convert
``insignificant'' into a bound by reporting the minimum detectable effect at 80\%
power for each key coefficient \citep{abadie2020}. None of these steps uses the
reserved hand-coded motive; all use observable data only.

\section{Main Results}\label{sec:res}
\paragraph{The average reaction (H1).} Table~\ref{tab:cars} and
Figure~\ref{fig:path} give the event-study result. The market marks the stock
\emph{down} after a WARN notice: mean CAR of $-0.9\%$ over $[0,+1]$, $-1.3\%$ over
$[0,+2]$ ($t=-2.22$), and $-1.6\%$ over $[0,+5]$ ($t=-2.41$). The drift is
post-event---the $[-10,-2]$ placebo is an insignificant $-0.18\%$---consistent
with the notice conveying news rather than being anticipated. But the reaction is
not universal: fewer than half of events ($49\%$) have negative CAR$[0,+5]$, so
the negative mean reflects a tail of badly-received layoffs rather than a
uniform markdown. H1 is supported (the average is significantly negative) with the
caveat that the median firm's stock barely moves.

\paragraph{Materiality, not headcount (H2).} Table~\ref{tab:main} is the core
cross-section. Relative layoff size is the variable that matters: alone (column 1)
a one-SD larger relative layoff is worth $-3.3$pp of CAR ($t=-2.77$). Firm size
enters with the mirror-image sign: a one-SD larger firm is worth $+3.1$pp
($t=3.34$, column 3). Absolute headcount is a precise zero (column 2, $R^2=0.000$,
$t=0.46$): the market does not react to the \emph{number} laid off, only to its
\emph{proportion} of the firm. Because relative-layoff and firm size are two
encodings of the same materiality, they partly compete when entered together
(column 4: relative layoff $-2.6$pp, $t=-1.87$; size $+1.5$pp, $t=1.53$), but the
materiality channel is unmistakable. The subgroup means in Table~\ref{tab:groups}
and Figure~\ref{fig:groups} tell the same story without any modeling: small firms
$-3.9\%$ ($t=-2.86$) versus large firms $+0.4\%$ (ns); top-half relative layoffs
$-3.5\%$ ($t=-2.70$) versus bottom-half $+0.2\%$ (ns). H2 is strongly supported.

\paragraph{Motive is not in the flag (H3).} The closure indicator---our
administrative proxy for strategic exit/reallocation as opposed to
cost-cutting---is economically and statistically zero throughout. Closures earn
$-1.5\%$ and ongoing-operation layoffs $-1.6\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:groups}); in the
regression the closure dummy is $-0.001$ ($t=-0.07$, Table~\ref{tab:main} column
5), and it stays insignificant in every robustness cut (Table~\ref{tab:robust}).
The market clearly \emph{does} discriminate among layoffs---by materiality---but
the closure-vs-layoff category available in administrative data does not capture
the discrimination. H3 is \emph{not} supported \emph{with the machine proxy}. As
Section~\ref{sec:power} shows, this is not because we have precisely estimated a
zero motive effect; it is because the machine proxy is too coarse to detect one.
That is the paper's pivotal negative result: if motive matters (as theory and the
strategy literature argue), it is encoded in the announcement narrative, not in
the WARN type field, and only hand-coding can recover it.

\section{Heterogeneity: Where the Bite Is}\label{sec:het}
\paragraph{The punishment is a small-firm phenomenon.} Table~\ref{tab:hetero}
shows the materiality effect is concentrated among small firms. Interacting
relative layoff with a below-median-size dummy, the relative-layoff slope is a
positive $+0.041$ ($t=2.08$) among large firms and the small-firm interaction is
$-0.065$ ($t=-2.86$)---so among large firms a bigger relative cut is, if anything,
mildly \emph{rewarded} (an efficiency read), while among small firms it is
punished (a demand read). Neither the closure nor the tech interaction is
significant. That a given relative cut flips sign with firm size is itself
suggestive that \emph{motive} is being inferred differently across firms---and is
a further reason to measure motive directly.

\paragraph{Not concentrated in any other observable subgroup.} If the materiality
effect were really a proxy for some other firm characteristic, it should
concentrate in the corresponding observable subgroup. Table~\ref{tab:heteroext}
interacts the standardized relative-layoff ratio with four additional pre-event,
observable moderators---high market-to-assets (glamour/growth), high asset
turnover, an early-sample (2022) indicator, and a goods-producing (non-tech
manufacturing) indicator---always controlling for firm size. In every case the
materiality slope is negative (ranging $-0.018$ to $-0.026$) and the interaction is
economically small and statistically indistinguishable from zero (the largest
$|t|$ on any interaction is $0.58$). The materiality effect is thus a broad
phenomenon, not a growth-firm, capital-intensity, sample-period, or
industry-specific artifact. Crucially, we do \emph{not} interact the reaction with
the hand-coded motive here---that dimension is unobservable to us and is the
student's reserved contribution; the moderators above are observable firm
characteristics chosen precisely because they are orthogonal to that reserved test.

\section{Randomization Inference}\label{sec:ri}
Cross-sectional CAR regressions with clustered standard errors rely on asymptotics
in the number of clusters, and event-induced variance can inflate conventional
test statistics \citep{boehmer1991,kolari2010}. Table~\ref{tab:randinf} and
Figure~\ref{fig:ri} report design-based inference from 2{,}000 permutations of the
regressor under the sharp null of no cross-sectional relation. The materiality
slope ($-0.024$ in the size-controlled specification) lies far outside the
permutation distribution (95\% permutation interval $[-0.014,+0.013]$; design-based
$p=0.001$): the relation between relative layoff size and CARs is not a
clustered-SE artifact. By contrast, the machine-motive (closure) coefficient
($-0.001$) sits essentially at the center of its permutation distribution
($p=0.92$, 95\% interval $[-0.031,+0.030]$)---the motive null is robust to
design-based inference. One caveat sharpens rather than weakens the reading: our
permutation reassigns the regressor at the event level and so ignores the
within-firm dependence that the clustered standard errors (328 firms, 615 events)
are designed to handle. Randomization inference and the clustered $t$ therefore
\emph{bracket} the true inference---the design-based $p=0.001$ is a lower bound on
significance and the size-controlled clustered $t=-1.81$ an upper bound---and the
materiality effect is significant on the unconditional clustered $t=-2.77$ as well. Randomization inference thus corroborates both halves of
the paper: the materiality effect is a genuine signal, and the machine-motive null
is real rather than a failure of the parametric standard errors. It does not,
however, tell us whether the motive null is a \emph{tight} bound; that is the
question the power analysis in Section~\ref{sec:power} answers, and its answer is
what makes the hand-coding necessary.

\section{Alternative Abnormal-Return Benchmarks}\label{sec:altbench}
The headline CARs use a firm-specific market model. To rule out that either the
average reaction or the materiality slope is an artifact of that benchmark, we
re-derive CAR$[0,+5]$ straight from the raw CRSP daily returns under three
alternatives: market-adjusted returns (abnormal return $=R_{it}-R_{mt}$, i.e.\
$\beta$ forced to one), S\&P-500-adjusted returns ($R_{it}-R^{\text{S\&P}}_{t}$),
and mean-adjusted returns ($R_{it}$ less the firm's estimation-window mean). As a
correctness check, the code first rebuilds the market-model CAR from the stored
$\alpha,\beta$ and confirms it matches the base CARs to machine precision
($\max|\text{diff}|<10^{-15}$ across 571 events). Table~\ref{tab:altbench} reports,
for each benchmark, the average CAR (H1) and the materiality slope (H2). The
average negative reaction is not only robust but \emph{stronger} against the two
index benchmarks that do not net out firm $\beta$: $-2.0\%$ ($t=-3.03$)
market-adjusted and $-2.0\%$ ($t=-3.06$) S\&P-adjusted, versus $-1.6\%$
($t=-2.41$) for the market model. It is weaker and marginally insignificant under
the mean-adjusted model ($-1.0\%$, $t=-1.53$)---expected, because mean-adjustment
ignores the generally negative market drift over some event windows and so is the
noisiest benchmark. The materiality slope is stable and negative across all four
benchmarks ($-0.020$ to $-0.027$; $|t|$ up to $2.08$). The core results survive
the choice of abnormal-return model.

\section{Is the Motive Null Informative? Power and Minimum Detectable Effects}\label{sec:power}
A null is only interesting if the design could have detected a real effect, and
here the answer differs sharply between the materiality slope and the motive proxy.
Table~\ref{tab:power} reports, for each key coefficient, the minimum detectable
effect at 80\% power, $\text{MDE}=(z_{0.975}+z_{0.80})\,\widehat{\text{SE}}\approx
2.80\,\widehat{\text{SE}}$. For the materiality slope the estimate ($-2.5$pp per SD
in the size-controlled specification, $-3.3$pp unconditionally) is comparable to
its $\approx3.9$pp 80\%-power MDE: the design is powered to detect roughly the
effect it actually finds. That the size-controlled clustered $t$ is only $-1.81$
reflects the mechanical relative-layoff/size collinearity flagged above, not a
fragile effect---the unconditional slope is significant at $t=-2.77$ and the
randomization-inference $p$ is $0.001$ (Section~\ref{sec:ri}). The average reaction
is likewise a clear but not high-powered signal (estimate $-1.6$pp, MDE $1.9$pp;
significant at $t=-2.41$ because significance requires $1.96\times$SE while the
80\%-power MDE is $2.80\times$SE). The machine-motive proxy is a different case
entirely. Its coefficient is a near-perfect zero ($-0.1$pp), but its standard error is
large (1.8pp), so the smallest motive effect it could have detected at 80\% power
is \emph{5.1 percentage points}---larger than the entire average market reaction to
a layoff and far above any plausible motive-driven CAR difference in the strategy
literature. In other words, the closure flag does not deliver an informative null:
it is both a point estimate of zero \emph{and} too imprecise to rule out an
economically meaningful motive effect. This is the quantitative case for
hand-coding. A sharper, narrative-based motive measure would (i) replace a coarse
binary that mixes cost-cutting and reallocation within the ``closure'' category
with a clean strategic-vs-defensive split, and (ii) plausibly reduce the noise that
inflates the current MDE, so that a true motive effect of, say, 1--3pp---below the
current $5.1$pp detection threshold but consistent with the layoff literature---could
actually be detected. The design has been built to receive exactly that measure.

\section{Robustness}\label{sec:robust}
Table~\ref{tab:robust} re-estimates the full specification under alternative
choices. The relative-layoff effect is stable and negative across dependent
variables (CAR$[0,+2]$: $-0.031$, $t=-2.01$; CAR$[-1,+5]$: $-0.028$, $t=-2.04$),
when 2022 events are dropped ($-0.030$, $t=-1.73$), when we restrict to
U.S.-incorporated firms ($-0.025$, $t=-1.75$), and when firm size is measured by
market capitalization instead of assets ($-0.029$, $t=-2.15$). The firm-size
coefficient is consistently positive, attenuating to insignificance only when
relative layoff (its near-mirror) is also in the model---an expected
multicollinearity, not a fragility. The closure (motive) proxy is insignificant in
all six columns. Together with the alternative-benchmark table
(Table~\ref{tab:altbench}) and the randomization-inference table
(Table~\ref{tab:randinf}), the materiality result is robust to window, sample,
size proxy, abnormal-return benchmark, and inference method; the null on the
machine-coded motive is equally robust---and, per Section~\ref{sec:power}, equally
uninformative as a bound.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}
Using a new matched WARN-to-CRSP sample, we find that the market's reaction to a
mass-layoff notice is, on average, a modest negative $-1.6\%$ over five days
(robust, and stronger, under alternative benchmarks), but that this average is
almost entirely about \emph{materiality}: layoffs that are large relative to a
small firm are punished ($-3.9\%$), while the same administrative event at a large
firm is a non-event. The number of workers laid off, per se, carries no
information, and randomization inference confirms the materiality effect is a
genuine signal ($p=0.001$) rather than a standard-error artifact. Most importantly
for the question in our title, the one motive proxy available in administrative
data---plant closure versus ongoing-operation layoff---does \emph{not} distinguish
the CARs, and the power analysis shows this is not a tight null: the machine proxy
could only have detected a motive effect of $5.1$ percentage points, so it cannot
rule out the economically meaningful motive effects the strategy literature
predicts. Whether a layoff is cost-cutting or strategic reallocation is not legible
in the WARN category field. The natural and necessary next step, which this paper
is designed to enable, is to read the announcements and \emph{hand-code} the
motive, then re-run the cross-section on it: only then can we answer whether the
market truly punishes cost-cutting and rewards reallocation, or vice versa.
Limitations include the three-state footprint (CA, TX, OR), the lead-name matching
(hand-verification pending), the 2022--2024 return window, and the coarse motive
proxy---each of which the student work is built to address.
